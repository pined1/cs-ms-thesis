5.1 The Question

Does the shape of how teams are connected affect how well incident knowledge spreads? H4 investigates whether network structure is a meaningful lever for organizational reliability.

Intuition: In a hub-spoke org (star topology), peripheral teams are 2 hops from each other — signal decays twice before arriving. In a distributed org (complete or WS), most teams are 1 hop apart — signal arrives at full strength. The same pipeline, same learning parameters, same incident rate — but the organizational wiring determines how much knowledge actually reaches each team. We expect more connected topologies to produce fewer incidents, and star topology to perform worst.

5.2 Why Topology Matters: Signal Decay

Signal decay was introduced in Section 1.3. Here we examine how it interacts with topology. The key interaction is simple: denser, more distributed topologies minimize average path length, which minimizes signal decay, which maximizes Stage 1 acquisition probability across the organization. As introduced in Section 1.3, acquisition probability degrades with each network hop under NEIGHBOR conditions: 

p_acquire = acquisition_prob × signal_decay ^ path_length

With signal_decay = 0.8 and acquisition_prob = 0.9:

Path LengthCalculationAcquisition Probability
1 hop (direct neighbor)0.9 × 0.8¹0.720
2 hops0.9 × 0.8²0.576
3 hops0.9 × 0.8³0.461

5.3 Why the NEIGHBOR Scenario

Topology is only a relevant factor under NEIGHBOR conditions. Under LOCAL, network structure is irrelevant since no information travels beyond the originating team. Under GLOBAL, network structure is irrelevant since information reaches everyone regardless of connectivity. Under a NEIGHBOR strategy, a team's neighborhood is defined by its network connections. NEIGHBOR is also the most realistic condition for a 20-team software organization: knowledge rarely stays entirely local, and rarely broadcasts organization-wide.

5.4 The Five Network Topologies

5.4.1 Complete Graph — 273 Incidents

[A]---[B]---[C] | \ / | \ / | | X | X | | / \ | / \ | [D]---[E]---[F] (every node connects to every other node)

What it is: Every team is directly connected to every other team. With 20 teams, this means 190 unique connections. Real-world analog: A tiny startup where everyone attends every meeting — there are no silos. Result: Every team is exactly one hop from the source of every incident. Prevention K = 0.990 — if one team sees a failure, all teams benefit.

5.4.2 Erdős-Rényi Random Graph — 323 Incidents

[A]---[B] [C] | / | [D]---[E]-[F] | \ | [G]-[H] (connections formed with 30% probability)

What it is: Each of the 190 possible team pairs is connected with probability p = 0.3. Real-world analog: Relationships that formed by chance — accumulated coincidence rather than deliberate org design. Result: ER outperforms WS not because randomness is superior to structure, but because density wins. The lesson is that connection count matters independently of topology pattern.

5.4.3 Watts-Strogatz Small-World — 336 Incidents (Baseline)

[1]-[2]-[3]-[4]-[5] | | [20] ring with [6] | shortcuts | [19] [7] ...

What it is: Teams are arranged in a conceptual ring, each connected to its 4 nearest neighbors. Then, 10% of connections are randomly rewired to create long-range shortcuts. Real-world analog: A 20-team software company with a squad structure — tightly-knit sub-groups with a few informal cross-team relationships. This is the most realistic topology and serves as the baseline. K = 0.890 indicates strong but incomplete organizational learning.

5.4.4 Barabási-Albert Hub-Spoke — 346 Incidents

[Hub-1] / | | \ \ [A][B][C][D][E] | [Hub-2] / | \ [F] [G] [H] (new nodes attach preferentially to well-connected nodes)

What it is: Teams join the network one at a time, each connecting to ba_m = 2 existing teams with probability proportional to those teams' existing degree (the "rich get richer" mechanism). Result: 2–3 hub teams with 10–12 connections each, and 12–14 peripheral teams with only 2–3 connections. Real-world analog: The platform team or SRE function that every product team depends on. All product teams have a relationship with platform; product teams have few relationships with each other. Knowledge concentrates at hubs but cannot efficiently diffuse outward to the spoke majority.

The ba_m crossover: A parameter sweep reveals a crossover at ba_m = 3. Below this value, BA performs worse than WS. Above ba_m = 3, BA begins to outperform WS as hubs become genuine accelerators.

5.4.5 Star — 382 Incidents (Worst)

[Hub] / | | \ [A][B][C][D]...[S] (all 19 spokes connect only to hub)

What it is: One team serves as the central hub. All 19 other teams connect exclusively to the hub. No spoke connects to any other spoke. Real-world analog: Extreme centralization — every incident debrief goes through the SRE lead, no peer-to-peer incident sharing. K = 0.670, the lowest of any topology. When a spoke team experiences a failure, its neighborhood consists of the hub and zero other spokes. Knowledge concentrates at the center and never diffuses to where incidents predominantly occur.

5.6 Does Every Team Need to Know About Every Incident?

The model handles this through cosine similarity filtering. Each incident has a feature vector; each team has a competency profile. The transformation probability is scaled by the cosine similarity between team and incident. A frontend team that encounters a deep storage incident will have low similarity, and therefore a low transformation probability.

5.7 Conclusion

Under the conditions modeled using NEIGHBOR sharing with 20 teams, network topology alone accounts for a 40% range in total incidents — from 273 (complete graph) to 382 (star) — with identical agents, identical incident rates, and identical learning parameters. H4 is supported: topology is a meaningful determinant of organizational reliability in this model.

The findings are directionally consistent with network theory (Watts & Strogatz, 1998; Reagans & McEvily, 2003): evenly distributed connectivity outperforms hub-spoke structures, and extreme centralization (star topology) is the worst-performing configuration. Whether these magnitudes hold in real software organizations depends on whether real knowledge flow follows network adjacency as closely as this model assumes — a simplification that future empirical work could test.
