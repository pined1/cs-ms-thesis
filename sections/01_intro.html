1.1 The Central Problem

Software systems are inherently prone to failure. From server outages and database unavailability to broken production deployments, these events are a routine reality for organizations running software at scale. The factor that distinguishes a high-reliability organization from their peers is not whether they have incidents, but how they utilize those failures to improve their systems afterwards.

Most engineering organizations hold postmortems: structured retrospectives where teams document what went wrong, why it went wrong, and what they will change to prevent recurrence. A more nuanced and less understood question is whether the breadth of postmortem knowledge distribution actually impacts organizational outcomes.

If Team A experiences a database failure and writes a detailed postmortem, should only Team A read it? Should Team A's direct neighbors in the organization also read it? Should the entire company? Broader sharing intuitively seems better, but there is a real cost: meeting time, cognitive load, alert fatigue, and the risk of overwhelming teams with incidents that feel irrelevant to them. The tradeoff is real, and no rigorous simulation study has examined how different sharing strategies actually affect organizational reliability outcomes. This thesis addresses that gap.

1.2 Why This Question Matters

Despite industry-wide investment in postmortem processes, the literature on how sharing scope affects reliability outcomes has not been widely discussed. Organizational learning theory (Levitt & March 1988; Argote 1999) holds that knowledge must flow through an organization to be useful. Absorptive capacity theory (Cohen & Levinthal 1990; Zahra & George 2002) explains that receiving knowledge is not enough — teams must also have the capacity to absorb and apply it.

These two stages map onto a distinction this simulation takes seriously: acquisition versus assimilation. Acquisition is the moment a team reads a postmortem and registers that it exists and is relevant to them — they have seen it, logged it, flagged it as worth knowing about. That is all acquisition is. It does not mean the team understands the failure deeply or knows how to act on it. That understanding is assimilation, which is Stage 2 in this model and depends on the team's prior knowledge and absorptive capacity. A team can acquire a postmortem without ever truly assimilating it, and this distinction matters: the simulation models each stage separately because collapsing them would obscure exactly the dynamic we are trying to study.

These theories have not been tested in the specific context of software incident knowledge sharing with a formal computational model. This simulation study provides that test, bridging organizational learning theory and absorptive capacity theory in a setting grounded in real engineering practice.

1.3 What We Built: The Agent-Based Model

The model is reflective of an organization, with twenty software engineering teams arranged in a social network — some teams are adjacent, some are distant in their communication. Each team owns a subsystem of the software system, and those subsystems generate incidents over time according to a stochastic process every day. This study uses 20 teams as the base configuration; the model supports arbitrary team counts.

An analogy: a technology company with 20 engineering teams, each team owns a set of software services and those services experience outages. After each outage, the team writes a postmortem. Now, the question is: should the postmortem findings stay just within the team that experienced the outage, be shared with adjacent teams from their communication network, or broadcast across the entire engineering organization? Our model simulates exactly this decision, run 100 times over a simulated year, under four different sharing strategies.

The simulation was designed as an agent-based model (ABM), in which individual agents (teams) follow rules and their aggregate behavior produces emergent organizational outcomes. ABMs are well suited to this problem because they allow for the kinds of complex, unplanned patterns that arise in real organizations. This makes ABMs particularly useful for studying questions where the answer cannot be predicted from the rules alone.

Source Asymmetry and the Pipeline Delay

When an incident occurs on Team A, Team A is deliberately skipped from stages 2–4 of the learning pipeline. The rationale is straightforward: Team A already learned from living through the incident. Routing them through the standard acquisition-assimilation-application pipeline would double-count their learning and inflate their knowledge score artificially. This is a design decision, not a bug.

The flip side of this asymmetry is equally important: the source team's learning is immediate. They experienced the incident in real time, and their knowledge is updated on the same day it occurred. Every other team that learns from the postmortem must pass through all four pipeline stages before their behavior changes — and that takes time. This pipeline delay is intentional: it models the real organizational gap between "an incident happened somewhere in the org" and "our team has actually changed how we work because of it." Reading a postmortem is not the same as internalizing and applying it, and the four-stage pipeline enforces that distinction at every step.

The delay compounds with network distance. Under NEIGHBOR sharing, a team that is 2 hops from the source faces two compounding disadvantages: a lower acquisition probability (because signal decays with each hop) and the same full four-stage pipeline once they do acquire the postmortem. A team that is farther away is therefore both less likely to receive the knowledge and slower to act on it once received. This is not a model limitation — it is a structural feature that reflects how knowledge actually travels through hierarchical or loosely-connected organizations.

This asymmetry also explains one of the study's more counterintuitive results: under LOCAL sharing, the transformation rate is 0%. LOCAL means only the source team knows about the incident — and the source team is the one team excluded from the pipeline. There is no recipient left to transform the postmortem into applied knowledge.

One key mechanism governs how knowledge travels under NEIGHBOR conditions: signal decay. Each hop an incident postmortem travels through the network reduces the acquisition probability of the receiving team: 

p_acquire = acquisition_prob × signal_decay ^ path_length

Under GLOBAL sharing, every team is treated as path_length = 1 regardless of their actual network position — the signal does not decay with distance. Under NEIGHBOR sharing, only teams that are direct connections (path_length = 1) receive the postmortem at all, and the decay formula applies to those transmissions. Signal decay is introduced here because it is a structural property of the NEIGHBOR strategy; its effect on organizational outcomes is examined directly in H4.

1.4 The 4-Stage Absorptive Capacity Pipeline

The backbone of the learning model comes from the theory presented by Zahra & George's (2002) reformulation of absorptive capacity, which describes how organizations acquire, process, and use external knowledge. Rather than treating learning as a single binary event, the model implements a four-stage pipeline through which incident knowledge must pass before it changes a team's behavior.

Stage 1 — Acquisition: Does the team receive the incident knowledge? A team that is not in the sharing scope for an incident never even sees the postmortem. Teams within scope receive the incident with a probability that reflects their current communication network.

Stage 2 — Assimilation: Does the team that has passed the acquisition stage understand the knowledge? Even when a team receives a postmortem, they may not fully grasp its implications for their own systems. Assimilation probability is influenced by the team's existing knowledge base.

Stage 3 — Transformation: Does the team connect the new knowledge to what they already know? Transformation is the cognitive work of integration. This stage has the highest failure point in the model, reflecting real-world evidence that cross-domain knowledge transfer is difficult.

Stage 4 — Exploitation: Does the team actually change its behavior to reduce future incidents? This is the stage at which reliability outcomes are measured. A team that fully exploits an incident's lesson lowers the base incident rate for the relevant subsystems.

Taken together, the four stage probabilities define the organization's learning velocity — how fast incident knowledge travels from postmortem to behavioral change. A team with high acquisition probability but low transformation probability acquires many postmortems but converts few into structural improvements. An organization's overall learning velocity is the product of all four stage rates, which is why sharing scope (which determines who even enters Stage 1) dominates the other parameters: a team that never acquires a postmortem cannot benefit from high assimilation or exploitation rates.

Transformation vs. Exploitation — Concrete Distinction
Stage Concrete Example
Transformation A team reads a database postmortem and realizes their own connection pool configuration has the same vulnerability — they mentally connect it to their system. Cognitive connection made, structural understanding updated; no action taken yet.
Exploitation The team actually changes the connection pool configuration, updates the runbook, and adds a monitoring alert — behavior changes, and incident risk drops.
Worked Example: On simulation day 47, Team A suffers a database connection pool exhaustion. In the NEIGHBOR condition, the adjacent Team B receives the post-mortem, while Team C—who is not adjacent—does not. Team B assimilates this information by drawing on their prior knowledge of similar systems, transforms it by applying the lessons to their own query timeout configurations, and exploits it by proactively updating their connection pool limits and monitoring alerts. In contrast, under the LOCAL condition, only Team A processes this pipeline. Under GLOBAL, all 19 other teams receive the report, each processing it based on their individual probabilities.

1.6 The Four Hypotheses

Hypothesis Factor Under Test Predicted Outcome
H1 — Sharing Scope How widely incident knowledge is shared after a failure GLOBAL sharing produces the fewest incidents; NONE produces the most. The expected ordering: NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL.
H2 — Deployment Velocity How frequently teams ship code to production The faster the deployment rate, the higher the incident rate — more deployments create more opportunities for failure.
Note on terminology — Exploitation (Stage 4) is the stage where a team translates accumulated knowledge into actual system changes: runbook updates, configuration fixes, and monitoring improvements. The parameter P_exploit controls the probability that a team successfully completes this stage on a given timestep.
H3 — Exploitation Effectiveness How intensely teams act on the knowledge they hold Early improvements in how teams use knowledge produce the biggest gains. As effectiveness increases, each additional unit of effort saves fewer incidents — diminishing returns.
H4 — Network Density How many connections exist between teams The more interconnected the organization, the faster knowledge spreads and the fewer total incidents occur.

A Note on H3 — Why Diminishing Returns?

In software reliability terms: moving a team from zero learning to some learning prevents a lot of incidents. Moving them from good to excellent at applying knowledge saves only a little more, because the easy wins are already gone, and the remaining risk is harder to eliminate.

1.7 Preview of Key Findings

Key Finding: Global incident knowledge sharing reduces organizational incidents by 45% compared to no sharing. This effect holds across all robustness tests (Cohen's d = 11.51, p < 0.001). All four hypotheses received support from the data.
