7.1 The Central Question

Section 6 applied ablation methodology: components were removed to test whether H1 depends on them. Experiment 13 is a different kind of experiment entirely — it is a cost-accounting sensitivity test that adds an economic measurement layer on top of the unchanged H1 simulation to ask whether the reliability gains from Section 2 translate into net engineering-hour savings.

Nothing is ablated here. The agent learning rules, knowledge decay function, source asymmetry routing, and sharing-scope architecture are all identical to the primary H1 configuration. Exp13 introduces two cost parameters that track engineering time expenditure in two distinct buckets — firefighting and learning overhead — but those parameters record outcomes; they do not feed back into agent behavior. Section 7.6 verifies this explicitly: setting the learning cost parameter to 0.0 produces identical incident counts, confirming that the cost-tracking layer is a measurement instrument, not a feedback mechanism.

The two cost parameters introduced in exp13 are: 

firefighting_cost_base = 4 engineering hours per incident
  (scales with severity and duration)
learning_cost_per_event = 2 engineering hours per successful learning event

More sharing produces more learning events, increasing learning overhead. It also builds more prevention knowledge, reducing future incidents and the firefighting hours they consume. These two effects run in opposite directions across the 365-day simulation. The central question exp13 asks is: do the H1 reliability gains — a 45% incident reduction under GLOBAL versus no-sharing — survive as net engineering-hour savings after accounting for the time cost of reading and processing postmortems organization-wide? Or does the overhead of global knowledge sharing cancel out the firefighting benefit it produces?

7.2 Two Buckets of Cost

Exp13 tracks engineering time in two separate buckets. Neither changes how agents behave — they are measurement instruments layered on top of the unchanged H1 simulation.

Cost Bucket 1 — Firefighting (incident response): When a production incident occurs, engineers stop other work to detect, diagnose, and resolve it. The cost scales with incident severity and resolution time: 

firefighting_cost = engineering_cost_base × severity_scale × duration_scale
  where: engineering_cost_base = 4 hours

Cost Bucket 2 — Learning overhead (postmortem processing): When a team successfully completes all four pipeline stages for an incident — acquiring the postmortem, assimilating it, transforming it into applicable knowledge, and exploiting it — that team incurs a learning cost: 

learning_cost = 2.0 hours per successful learning event (per team per incident)

Under GLOBAL sharing, up to 19 non-source teams can each log a 2.0-hour learning event per incident. Under LOCAL sharing, at most one team (the source) logs any learning event. This means GLOBAL sharing generates substantially more learning overhead — but it also generates the fastest Prevention Knowledge accumulation, which reduces future incident frequency and therefore future firefighting costs. These two effects run in opposite directions throughout the simulation.

7.3 How the Cost Dynamics Evolve Over Time

Under GLOBAL sharing, Prevention Knowledge saturates near K = 0.992 by approximately day 90 of the 365-day simulation. This creates a distinctive time-profile for costs:

  • Days 1–90 (accumulation phase): Learning events are frequent — teams are processing postmortems and building knowledge. Learning overhead is at its highest. Incidents are still occurring at a meaningful rate, so firefighting costs remain elevated.
  • Days 91–365 (harvest phase): Prevention Knowledge is near-saturated. Incident frequency drops sharply — fewer incidents means fewer firefighting events AND fewer new learning events (nothing new to process). Both cost types fall simultaneously. The 275-day harvest phase converts the 90-day learning investment into avoided-firefighting savings.
Cost structure: Learning investment is front-loaded and self-limiting. Prevention benefit is persistent. The 90-day accumulation phase funds 275 days of compounding returns.

7.5 The Return on Investment Calculation

Within this model, under these specific cost parameters, the exchange rate between learning investment and firefighting avoidance can be computed directly from the GLOBAL row:

GLOBAL learning economics (model output, not empirical claim):
Learning investment: ~580 engineering hours
Firefighting avoided (vs. NONE): ~1,361 engineering hours
Net savings: ~781 engineering hours per year, per 20-team organization
Exchange rate: every 1 hour invested in learning avoids approximately 2.3 hours of firefighting

This ratio is a function of the cost parameters chosen (engineering_cost_base = 4 hrs, learning_cost_per_event = 2.0 hrs) and the incident reduction magnitude from H1 (45%). Organizations with different cost profiles — shorter mean resolution times, higher postmortem overhead — would obtain a different exchange rate. The value of this result is not the specific ratio; it is the demonstration that, under a plausible parameter regime, the learning overhead does not cancel the H1 reliability gains. The net benefit is positive at every level of sharing scope.

7.6 Model Integrity: Learning Cost as Pure Accounting

In agent-based models, any parameter that touches the simulation state risks creating unintended feedback loops. When a cost parameter is introduced, a rigorous reviewer immediately asks: does this parameter modify agent behavior, or does it only record it? If learning_cost_per_event caused agents to reduce their pipeline participation — for example, by lowering P_exploit when costs accumulated past a threshold — then the cost model would be distorting the results it is supposed to measure. That would be a model design flaw, not a finding.

Exp13 includes an explicit isolation test to rule this out. The simulation was run with learning_cost_per_event = 0.0 (cost tracking disabled) and compared to the standard configuration (learning_cost_per_event = 2.0). Incident counts were identical across all four scenarios and all 100 seeds. This confirms that the cost-tracking layer is architecturally downstream of the agent state update loop: 

Each simulation tick (simplified):
  1. Generate incidents (stochastic, per-subsystem)
  2. Trigger post-incident learning pipeline (Stages 1–4, unchanged from H1)
  3. Update agent Prevention Knowledge vectors (unchanged from H1)
  4. Record cost: firefighting_cost += incident_cost; learning_cost += event_cost  ← measurement only

Steps 1–3 are identical whether cost tracking is on or off. Step 4 is a passive ledger entry. No cost value feeds back into the incident probability formula, the pipeline stage probabilities (P_acquire, P_assimilate, P_exploit), or the knowledge vector update.

Isolation verdict: Learning cost is a measurement instrument, not a model variable. The cost figures in Section 7.4 are properties of the H1 experiment — they describe what the simulation produces — not inputs that shaped it. The H1 results and the cost results are independently valid outputs of the same simulation run.

7.7 Conclusion

Under the cost parameters modeled — 4 engineering hours per incident, 2 hours per successful learning event — GLOBAL sharing produces a total engineering burden of approximately 1,189 hours per year across 20 teams, compared to 1,970 hours under no sharing. That is a 39.6% reduction in total cost. GLOBAL spends roughly 3× more on learning overhead than the no-sharing baseline (~580 vs. ~0 hours), yet achieves a 69% reduction in firefighting cost (609 vs. 1,970 hours). The learning investment compounds: the 90-day accumulation phase that produces knowledge saturation also reduces future learning overhead because fewer incidents means fewer postmortems to process.

Three clarifications matter for interpreting this result correctly:

  1. This is a model output, not an empirical measurement. The 2.3× exchange rate (1 hour of learning avoids 2.3 hours of firefighting) is a consequence of the chosen cost parameters and the H1 incident reduction magnitude. Real organizations with shorter resolution times, higher postmortem overhead, or lower incident rates would compute a different ratio. The conclusion to draw is not "global sharing returns 2.3× ROI" — it is that, within a plausible parameter regime, the learning overhead does not cancel the reliability gains.
  2. Cost tracking does not affect agent behavior. Section 7.6 confirms that setting learning cost to 0.0 produces identical incident counts. The cost figures are downstream measurements, not inputs. The H1 ordering — NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL — is unchanged by whether cost tracking is active.
  3. The ordering is monotone. Every step up in sharing scope (LOCAL → NEIGHBOR → GLOBAL) reduces total engineering cost in this model, even after accounting for the added learning overhead at each level. There is no crossover point where additional sharing becomes net-negative under these parameters.
