2. H1 — Learning Strategy Comparison

2.1 The Central Question

Does sharing incident knowledge more broadly reduce system failures? H1 states: GLOBAL > NEIGHBOR > LOCAL > NONE, where "greater" means fewer incidents and higher availability. This section covers the evidence for that ordering and examines how robust it is across a wide range of conditions.

The expected ordering is NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL in total incidents (inverse ordering in knowledge accumulation). If any reversal appeared in these results, it would indicate a model implementation error. Confirming this ordering is the primary purpose of this experiment.

2.2 A Real-World Analogy: The Postmortem Sharing Decision

When a software organization has to handle a production outage, the on-call team has to jump in now to investigate, resolve the issue, and eventually write a postmortem. That document is full of critical lessons: which configuration was wrong, what monitoring was missing, and how the failure cascaded through the system. The real question is what happens to those lessons once the incident is over.

If the written report stays in the owning team's private Confluence page, that is local learning — the knowledge is isolated to the team that lived through the failure. If the team posts it in a shared Slack channel that adjacent teams in the communication network follow, that is neighbor-level learning — the signal reaches teams close in the organization graph. If the team that wrote the report presents their findings in a company-wide incident review attended by all engineering teams, that is global learning — every team hears about it and can update their own practices accordingly.

My simulation follows this same logic, using the organization as the primary unit of measurement. The core mechanism is agent-level knowledge accumulation, which directly reduces the probability of future incidents. By adjusting the dissemination boundaries, I can test how different sharing scopes impact the organization's overall reliability.

2.3 The Four Scenarios

ScenarioWho Learns After an Incident
NONE Pure baseline. Incidents occur, nothing is updated.
LOCAL The team that owns the affected subsystem. An on-call engineer who fixes an outage, keeps the notes in a private doc, and never shares them with anyone else.
NEIGHBOR The source team which experienced the incident plus all teams directly connected to it in the network. Same as posting a postmortem in a shared Slack channel that adjacent teams follow.
GLOBAL Every team in the organization, regardless of proximity. Like a mandatory company-wide incident review where all engineering teams attend.

2.4 Experimental Setup

The simulation models an organization of 20 teams connected via a Watts-Strogatz small-world network, a topology chosen because it balances local clustering with short average path lengths — a characteristic of how real engineering organizations tend to be structured: tightly-knit subgroups with a few cross-cutting connections.

Each scenario ran for 365 simulated days across 100 independent random seeds. Running 100 seeds is equivalent to running the same organizational experiment in 100 parallel universes with the same starting conditions, allowing a distribution of outcomes rather than a single point estimate.

Mean Incidents How many system failures the organization experienced over one simulated year Count every failure across all 20 teams over 365 days. Run the simulation 100 times with different random seeds. Average the results. Lower is better. NONE = 484 failures/year. GLOBAL = 266 failures/year.
Availability The percentage of time the system was up and working normally, from an end-user perspective Each incident causes downtime based on how long it takes to detect and recover. Availability = 1 − (total downtime ÷ total time). Averaged across all 100 seeds. Higher is better. The gap between 98.29% (NONE) and 99.26% (GLOBAL) equals roughly 45 minutes less downtime per week per team.
Prevention K How much incident-prevention knowledge the organization has accumulated — a measure of organizational memory Each team holds a knowledge vector (see below). Prevention K is the average value across all dimensions of that vector, averaged across all 20 teams, at day 365. 0 = no knowledge accumulated. 1 = maximum possible knowledge. GLOBAL reaches 0.992 — near the ceiling. LOCAL reaches only 0.555.
Transform % How often incident knowledge produced a deep change in how a team operates — not just absorbing information, but actually restructuring behavior After each incident, the cosine similarity between the team's knowledge vector and the incident's feature vector is computed. If similarity exceeds the transformation threshold, a transformation event is recorded. Transform % = (transformation events ÷ total learning opportunities) × 100. 0% means teams absorbed information but never reached the threshold for structural change. 89.5% (GLOBAL) means nearly every incident produced real behavioral change across the organization.

2.6 The Transformation Rate Jump: Source Asymmetry

The 0% transformation rate in the LOCAL scenario is due to how LOCAL accumulates knowledge. In the simulation, the team that owns the affected subsystem is skipped in the post-incident learning pipeline — it is assumed to have learned implicitly by experiencing the failure. Under the LOCAL strategy, no other teams are in the learning pipeline, so the cross-team pipeline never fires and transformation events do not occur.

The NEIGHBOR strategy introduces at least one non-source team into every learning event. GLOBAL reaches 89.5% because every team in the organization received every incident's knowledge; cosine similarity then rises, the transformation threshold is crossed repeatedly, making structural learning the norm.

2.7 When Does the Ordering Emerge?

In the first 30 days of the simulation, H1 holds: the NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL incident ordering is already present and statistically distinguishable.

2.9 The Robustness of H1

  • Team count: 6, 20, and 50 teams
  • Simulation duration: 180 to 1,095 days
  • Incident rate: 0.01 to 0.20 daily probability per subsystem
  • Knowledge decay: half-lives ranging from approximately 2 weeks to 19 years
  • Documentation quality: from poor (high noise in knowledge transfer) to high fidelity

The sharing model was put through a series of stress tests to see if the results would change under different conditions. Everything was varied: from the size of the company to how quickly people forget what they learned — scale and time (6 teams to 50, 6 months to 3 years), environment (very stable to high-failure systems), and memory and quality (teams that forget lessons in two weeks versus those that remember for 19 years, and from noisy documentation to high-fidelity reports). The performance ordering (NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL) held in all cases — it is not a fluke of the base parameter settings but a stable property of how knowledge flows through the network under these model assumptions.

2.10 Conclusion

Under the conditions modeled — 20 teams, Watts-Strogatz topology, 365 simulated days, and a base incident rate of 0.10 — the data support H1. The ordering NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL in incident counts held across all 100 seeds and was statistically separable at each step (all Cohen's d > 3.44, p < 0.001). The 45% reduction from NONE to GLOBAL is large in absolute terms, but reflects specific model assumptions about how knowledge flows and decays. Whether this magnitude translates to real organizations depends on how well these assumptions hold in practice — a question for empirical follow-up. What the simulation establishes is that, under these assumptions, sharing scope is the dominant lever for reliability.
