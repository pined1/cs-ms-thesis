Part D: Conclusion

What We Built and Validated

This thesis produced a computational agent-based model of 20 software engineering teams sharing incident knowledge through a four-stage absorptive capacity pipeline — Acquisition, Assimilation, Transformation, and Exploitation — grounded in Zahra & George (2002). The simulation was not designed to prescribe organizational behavior; it was designed to provide a controlled experimental environment for studying how structural and behavioral variables interact in a knowledge-sharing system that would be difficult or impossible to manipulate in a live organization.

The model was pre-specified against three validation criteria before experiments were run: (1) H1 ordering emergence by simulation day 30, (2) Prevention Knowledge saturation by day 90 under the GLOBAL sharing condition, and (3) full topology ordering under the NEIGHBOR condition. All four experimental hypotheses were supported. The H1 ordering — GLOBAL < NEIGHBOR < LOCAL < NONE — held across 13 experimental conditions, 6 sensitivity sweeps, and 2 ablation tests. No parameter combination tested reversed this ordering.

The primary scientific contribution of this work is the simulator itself: a validated, reproducible computational tool that operationalizes absorptive capacity theory in a software incident learning context. The fact that the model reproduces theoretically predicted orderings provides evidence that it is a reasonable implementation of the underlying theory, and a credible basis for the exploratory findings described below.

What the Simulation Suggests

The following observations are specific to the model as implemented, under the assumptions encoded in it. They are offered as patterns worth investigating further, not as prescriptive conclusions.

  • Sharing scope (H1) is the dominant lever for reliability under the conditions modeled. Moving from no sharing to global sharing is associated with a 45% reduction in mean incident counts. This effect is large relative to other parameters tested and is robust to sensitivity variation in acquisition probability, exploitation effectiveness, and network density. These results are consistent with the theoretical claim that boundary-spanning knowledge transfer yields higher marginal returns than internal processing depth. Empirical follow-up is needed to determine whether this holds in real organizations and under what boundary conditions.
  • Network topology alone accounts for approximately 40% of variance in incident counts (H4), suggesting that organizational structure shapes learning outcomes independently of individual team behavior. Under these modeled conditions, star topologies consistently underperform distributed structures. This is suggestive of a structural bottleneck effect, though the mechanism has not been empirically confirmed outside the simulation.
  • Deployment velocity risk is strongly absorbed by organizational learning (H2). A 10× increase in deployment rate produced only a 24% increase in incidents under the GLOBAL sharing condition, compared to much larger increases under restricted sharing. This pattern is directionally consistent with findings from high-performing DevOps organizations (Forsgren et al., 2018), and suggests the simulator captures a plausible interaction effect. Whether this ratio holds empirically is an open question.
  • Stage 4 exploitation intensity (H3) shows diminishing returns at realistic parameter values. Under the range of exploitation effectiveness values tested (~0.3–0.7), the incident-count difference attributable to exploitation quality is approximately 4%. Under these modeled conditions, structural sharing investments appear to yield higher marginal returns than behavioral quality investments in documentation or postmortem analysis depth. This finding warrants caution before over-interpreting, as the model does not capture the full complexity of knowledge quality effects in real teams.
Summary: This study produces a validated computational implementation of absorptive capacity theory applied to software incident learning. Under the conditions modeled, sharing scope is the most robust predictor of reliability outcomes, and network topology matters independently of learning behavior. These patterns are suggestive and reproducible within the simulation; they are not organizational prescriptions. The open questions below identify the most promising directions for empirical follow-up and model extension.

11. Open Questions and Future Directions

The simulation findings described in this thesis are best understood as a set of structured hypotheses generated by a validated computational model, not as empirical conclusions about real organizations. This section translates the most policy-relevant simulation patterns into candidate empirical hypotheses, proposes specific follow-up experiments that could be run using this simulator, and identifies extensions that would increase the model's realism and scientific scope.

11.1 From Simulation Findings to Empirical Hypotheses

Each of the following patterns was observed consistently within the simulation under the conditions modeled. Each represents a testable claim about organizational behavior that existing simulation data cannot confirm on its own.

  • Sharing scope vs. documentation quality (from H1). The simulation suggests that broadening sharing scope yields higher marginal returns than improving the depth or quality of individual postmortem documentation. This could be tested with natural experiments comparing organizations that changed their postmortem distribution policies — for example, organizations that moved from team-local wikis to company-wide incident databases — while holding documentation format and training investments roughly constant. If the simulation pattern holds in practice, incident rates should decline more after policy changes that expand audience than after changes that improve documentation standards.
  • Hub-spoke topology and knowledge diffusion (from H4). The simulation suggests that star-topology organizational designs produce systematically worse knowledge diffusion than distributed structures. This is testable using organizational network analysis data collected from software engineering teams with identifiable knowledge-sharing structures. Organizations with high betweenness centralization in their communication graphs would be predicted, under this hypothesis, to show slower incident-learning curves than those with more distributed connectivity.
  • Deployment velocity and learning absorption (from H2). The simulation suggests that high-deployment organizations with strong knowledge sharing may absorb deployment risk more efficiently than those with restricted sharing. This is directionally consistent with DORA findings (Forsgren et al., 2018) linking elite performers to both high deployment frequency and low change failure rates. The mechanism proposed by the model — faster incident-knowledge propagation buffering the risk of each additional deployment — warrants mechanism-level empirical study, including longitudinal analysis of organizations undergoing CI/CD adoption alongside postmortem practice changes.

11.2 Suggested Experiments Using This Simulator

The simulator as implemented supports rapid parametric variation and is well-suited for the following controlled experiments, none of which were in scope for the original thesis but which follow directly from the current findings.

11.3 Model Extensions

The following extensions would increase the simulator's scientific credibility and expand the range of questions it can address:

  • Formal ODD protocol documentation. The model should be documented using the ODD (Overview, Design concepts, Details) protocol (Grimm et al., 2020) to support independent replication and submission to venues such as the Journal of Artificial Societies and Social Simulation (JASSS). The current documentation is sufficient for thesis purposes but does not meet the reproducibility standard expected for computational social science publication.
  • Scale-free validation with 50+ nodes. The current 20-team implementation produces Barabási-Albert topologies that are too small to exhibit true scale-free degree distributions. Rerunning experiments with 50 or more nodes would allow proper empirical testing of BA topology properties and more reliable H4 topology comparisons.
  • Heterogeneous teams. The current model assigns all teams identical base competencies and sizes. Introducing variation in team size, prior knowledge levels, and incident exposure rates would better reflect real software organizations and could reveal interaction effects not visible in the homogeneous baseline.
  • Dynamic knowledge decay. The model currently does not distinguish between codified knowledge (documented in postmortems) and tacit knowledge (held by individual engineers). Implementing differentiated decay rates for each type — codified knowledge decays more slowly but is less actionable; tacit knowledge decays faster with attrition — would address a known limitation of the absorptive capacity operationalization and make the model more sensitive to realistic turnover scenarios.
