6. Ablation Tests — exp11 (Knowledge Decay) & exp12 (Source Asymmetry)

6.1 What Is an Ablation Test?

A simulation model is built such that some components may be load-bearing: remove them, and the model's behavior changes in important ways. Others are robust: when removed, the results barely shift. An ablation test is how we tell the difference. The procedure is to disable one model component, re-run the full experiment under identical conditions, and compare the output to the default.

Experiments 11 and 12 each target a different component of the H1 learning model. Both were run under the same conditions as the primary H1 experiment: 20 teams, Watts-Strogatz small-world network, 365 simulated days, 100 independent random seeds, all four scenarios.

The H1 ordering being preserved across ablations is the key test — if removing decay or source asymmetry caused the ordering to collapse, that would indicate H1 results were an artifact of those specific components rather than a fundamental property of sharing scope.

The key diagnostic: if the H1 ordering (NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL) survives both ablations, the result is robust to those specific modeling assumptions. If the ordering collapses, H1 would need to be re-examined.

6.2 exp11 — Removing Knowledge Decay

The default simulation treats prevention knowledge as impermanent. Knowledge earned by a team on day 10 can slowly erode by day 200 if it is not reinforced, following an exponential decay function (decay rate = 0.001 per timestep). This models engineers leaving teams, practices not being enforced, and on-call rotation. The empirical grounding is Darr, Argote, and Epple's (1994) study of organizational forgetting in the service industry, which tracked production knowledge across franchise locations and found that accumulated knowledge decayed measurably during idle periods when it was not being reinforced.

In exp11, the knowledge decay rate is set to 0.0; knowledge earned at any point is retained in full.

ScenarioDefault (decay on)No Decay (exp11)Difference

LOCAL 406.4398.5−7.9 fewer
NEIGHBOR 336.0323.0−13.0 fewer (3.9%)

Why NEIGHBOR benefits most: Under the NEIGHBOR strategy, knowledge accumulates slowly, spreading outward from the source team one hop at a time over many timesteps. Because that accumulation is gradual and can spread across many teams at moderate levels, decay chips away at knowledge before it ever reaches a self-reinforcing peak. When decay is turned off, all that slowly accumulated knowledge is preserved in full. The GLOBAL strategy, by contrast, saturates organizational knowledge rapidly — every new incident reinforces what teams already know, so the organization is constantly re-learning. Knowledge that is actively replenished each day barely erodes.

Verdict: Knowledge decay is a meaningful component that affects the results in the expected direction, and its removal produces changes consistent with the model's logic. The H1 ordering (NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL) is preserved under both the default and no-decay conditions, indicating that H1 results are not an artifact of the knowledge decay function.

6.3 exp12 — Removing Source Asymmetry

In the model, source asymmetry is one of the more important design decisions in the four-stage learning pipeline. When an incident occurs at Team A (the source team), Team A is skipped in stages 2–4 of the post-incident learning pipeline. The rationale is that Team A owns the affected subsystem and has experienced the incident in real time. Routing Team A through the standard learning pipeline for its own incident would double-count its learning.

In exp12, the source team skip is removed, meaning the source team now goes through all four pipeline stages for its own incident, exactly like any other learner.

ScenarioDefault (asymmetry on)No Asymmetry (exp12)Difference

LOCAL gets substantially worse (+7.7%): In the default model, the team that experiences the incident goes through a direct, high-confidence learning path. When that path is removed (exp12), that team must follow the same lower-probability learning pipeline as every other learner. Because LOCAL relies entirely on the source team to carry organizational knowledge forward, removing this asymmetry substantially degrades performance — the organization's primary learning pathway is weakened.

GLOBAL barely changes (~0 incidents): With 20 teams learning from every incident, the difference attributable to one team's pipeline path is diluted to near-zero. GLOBAL's strength is precisely this redundancy.

Verdict: Source asymmetry is the most structurally revealing ablation in this study. It exposes the fragility of narrow, source-centric learning under the conditions modeled: a configuration that depends entirely on the incident-owning team to carry organizational knowledge forward. The H1 ordering (NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL) is preserved under both conditions, indicating that H1 results are not an artifact of the source asymmetry design choice.

6.4 Combined Conclusion: What exp11 and exp12 Together Establish

Together, the two ablations accomplish three things:

  1. They strengthen confidence in model validity. When a component is removed and results change in the direction and magnitude the model's logic predicts, that is evidence the model is behaving consistently with its own internal structure.
  2. They demonstrate robustness to design choices. Neither ablation reverses the H1 ordering. Under both ablations, the ordering (NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL) is a property of knowledge flow structure, not an artifact of decay rates or pipeline asymmetry.
  3. They provide mechanistic insight beyond the primary results. Exp12 illuminates why a LOCAL strategy exhibits a knowledge ceiling far below NEIGHBOR and GLOBAL under these modeled conditions.

7. Ablation — exp13 Learning Cost

7.1 The Central Question

Fewer incidents are desirable. But every postmortem review consumes engineering time. If global sharing substantially increases the number of learning events that occur, and each learning event carries a real time cost, could the overhead of learning outweigh the benefit of fewer failures?

Exp13 asks directly: does global knowledge sharing reduce total engineering cost, or does the learning overhead it generates cancel out the firefighting hours it saves?

7.2 Two Buckets of Cost

Cost Type 1 — Incident Response Cost (firefighting): When a production system fails, engineers drop what they are doing and restore it. Formula: 

engineering_cost_base (4 hrs) × severity_scale × duration_scale

Cost Type 2 — Learning Cost (investment): When teams move through the four-stage knowledge pipeline — reading postmortems, attending retrospectives, updating runbooks — they spend time tracked separately: 

learning_cost = 2.0 hours per successful learning event

More sharing produces more learning events, increasing learning cost. It also produces more knowledge capital, decreasing future incidents and firefighting cost. These two effects work against each other throughout the 365-day simulation.

7.3 How the Cost Dynamics Evolve Over Time

In the GLOBAL scenario, the learning cost is front-loaded. Prevention knowledge reaches near-saturation (k = 0.992) by approximately day 90. Incident rates drop sharply, and fewer incidents also mean fewer learning events, so learning overhead shrinks as well. By year's end, the learning investment was concentrated in those first 90 days, while the avoided-firefighting benefit accumulates across the remaining days of the simulation.

Core insight: The learning cost is front-loaded; the prevention benefit is persistent.

7.6 Model Integrity: Learning Cost as Pure Accounting

Setting the learning cost parameter to 0.0 produced identical incident counts to the standard configuration. This confirms that the cost-tracking layer is correctly isolated from the simulation's learning mechanics. Learning cost is a measurement instrument, not a feedback mechanism.

7.7 Conclusion

Under the conditions modeled, choosing not to invest in post-incident learning does not appear to be a cost-saving strategy — it functions as a cost-shifting strategy, moving engineering time from learning overhead into a larger firefighting burden. In this 20-team simulation, the difference in total engineering hours across scenarios is measurable and consistent across all 100 seeds.

8. Sensitivity Sweeps — Which Model Knobs Matter?

8.1 Sensitivity Sweeps vs. Ablation Studies

An ablation study removes a component entirely to test whether it contributes at all. A sensitivity sweep keeps the component active but varies its numeric value across a wide range, asking whether the findings would change if the model had been calibrated differently.

The critical threshold appears between 0.3 and 0.5: below 0.5, teams miss enough postmortems that incident counts rise sharply (~39 additional incidents). Above 0.5, the daily retry mechanism compensates and raising the rate further yields diminishing returns — moving from 0.9 to perfect (1.0) saves only 6 more incidents. H1 ordering holds across the full range tested.
