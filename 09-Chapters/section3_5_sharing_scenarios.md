# Section 3.5: The Four Sharing Scenarios

The independent variable of this study is the scope of postmortem knowledge sharing following each simulated incident. Four discrete conditions — NONE, LOCAL, NEIGHBOR, and GLOBAL — are defined not as points on an abstract continuum but as computational representations of organizational policies that can be directly observed in real software engineering environments. Each condition specifies which teams, if any, are permitted to enter the learning pipeline described in Section 3.4, and at what signal strength their acquisition probability is evaluated. Together, the four conditions span the full range from zero organizational learning to maximum knowledge diffusion, with two empirically grounded intermediate cases. This design follows the principle articulated by Kim et al. (2016) that high-performing organizations work to transform "local discoveries into global improvements" — a principle the four scenarios operationalize as a testable spectrum rather than an aspiration.

The NONE condition establishes the baseline floor of the experiment. Under NONE, every incident is logged and a postmortem record is created, but no team — including the incident source team — ever enters the learning pipeline. The postmortem exists as an artifact but is never read, processed, or acted upon. This condition represents organizations that treat each incident as an isolated operational failure rather than a learning opportunity: the incident is resolved, the ticket is closed, and institutional memory accrues only through the informal and unreliable channel of direct personal experience. The resulting knowledge stock trajectory is entirely determined by incident frequency and the background decay rate, with no corrective signal from prior events.

Under the LOCAL condition, only the incident source team enters the learning pipeline following each postmortem. Network neighbors and all other teams receive no signal. This condition arises in the simulation through source asymmetry: the source team is excluded from the set of teams eligible for cross-team sharing (stages 2 through 4 of the pipeline), and because the source team is also excluded from receiving its own postmortem as an external signal, the practical effect is that only the source team's internal review contributes to its knowledge update. No cross-team exposure occurs, and the Transform percentage — the fraction of simulation ticks on which at least one non-source team completes the Transformation stage — is 0%. The real-world analog is a team that writes a thorough postmortem and stores it in a private team wiki or internal folder inaccessible to peers. Knowledge accumulates through direct experience alone.

The NEIGHBOR condition extends sharing to the incident source team plus all teams that are direct network neighbors of the source — that is, all teams at path length 1. Adjacent teams receive the postmortem with an acquisition probability equal to the baseline acquisition_prob scaled by signal_decay^1. Teams at path length 2 or greater receive no signal. This represents a common middle-ground practice: posting a postmortem in a shared Slack channel or incident review forum that neighboring teams follow passively. Adjacent teams may or may not act on the posted document, modeled here through the stochastic acquisition probability. Under NEIGHBOR, cross-team exposure occurs but is structurally limited by the network topology, and the realized Transform percentage is 14% — a meaningful increase over LOCAL but far below what full diffusion achieves.

The GLOBAL condition represents the broadest possible sharing policy: every team in the organization receives the postmortem from every incident, regardless of network position or path length. For acquisition probability calculations, all receiving teams are treated as being at path length 1, reflecting the assumption that a well-structured, indexed, and actively distributed postmortem document is equally accessible to a geographically distant team as to an adjacent one. This assumption is theoretically grounded in Hansen's (1999) finding that weak ties are sufficient for the transfer of codified knowledge. Because postmortems are codified artifacts — structured documents with defined sections, explicit timelines, and enumerated action items — they do not require the repeated personal interaction or shared tacit context that makes weak-tie transfer of complex knowledge difficult. GLOBAL sharing is therefore not a utopian scenario requiring organizational trust that rarely exists; it is, as Lunney and Lueder (2016) document in Google's site reliability engineering practice, a concrete cultural and process commitment to blameless postmortem review that organizations have demonstrated they can sustain. The realized Transform percentage under GLOBAL is 89.5%. Edmondson's (1999) work on psychological safety is relevant here as well: the degree to which teams write honest, detailed postmortems — and the degree to which receiving teams engage with them rather than dismissing them — depends on whether the organizational environment supports candid disclosure of failure. GLOBAL sharing sets the structural conditions for diffusion; psychological safety determines the quality of the information that flows through those channels.

The four scenarios are summarized in Table 3.2.

**Table 3.2: The Four Sharing Scenarios**

| Scenario | Scope | Real-World Analog | Transform % |
|---|---|---|---|
| NONE | No sharing | No postmortem culture | 0% |
| LOCAL | Source team only | Private team wiki | 0% |
| NEIGHBOR | Source + direct neighbors | Shared Slack channel | 14% |
| GLOBAL | All 20 teams | Company-wide incident review | 89.5% |

The choice to use exactly four scenarios rather than a finer-grained continuum reflects a deliberate design priority: each condition must be interpretable as a recognizable organizational practice. A scenario defined as "teams within path length 1.5" would have no real-world referent and would obscure rather than illuminate the mechanisms under study. The four conditions chosen here correspond to policy decisions that an engineering organization can actually make — do nothing, keep it internal, share with neighbors, or share with everyone — and the results reported in Chapter 4 are interpretable in those terms.

## Committee Watch

**What the committee will scrutinize and how to answer:**

1. "Why is LOCAL Transform % = 0%? The source team still learns." The Transform % metric counts ticks on which a non-source team completes the Transformation stage. Under LOCAL, no non-source team ever enters the pipeline, so by definition this count is zero. The source team's own learning is captured in its knowledge stock trajectory, not in the Transform % metric. This is a measurement definition, not a claim that LOCAL produces no learning whatsoever.

2. "Why treat all GLOBAL teams as being at path_length = 1 rather than their actual network distance?" This is a modeling decision that reflects the nature of postmortems as codified documents. Once a postmortem is published to a company-wide forum, its accessibility does not degrade with network distance the way an informal conversation would. The path_length = 1 assignment is a proxy for "document is equally readable by all." The sensitivity of results to this assumption is a legitimate follow-up question; if pressed, note that weakening it (e.g., applying mild decay for distant teams) would reduce the GLOBAL Transform % but would not close the gap with NEIGHBOR, since topology still governs NEIGHBOR's reach regardless.

3. "Is GLOBAL realistic for large organizations?" The Hansen (1999) result on weak ties and codified knowledge directly addresses this. Additionally, Lunney and Lueder (2016) provide empirical evidence that large-scale organizations (Google, at the time of publication) have operationalized exactly this kind of mandatory cross-team postmortem review. The model is not claiming all organizations do this — only that the scenario is achievable, which the empirical literature confirms.

4. "Why not include a fifth scenario, such as path_length = 2?" The four scenarios were chosen to correspond to identifiable real-world practices. A path_length = 2 condition would fall between NEIGHBOR and GLOBAL in a way that lacks a clear organizational policy analog. Including it would add statistical comparisons without adding interpretive clarity. The current design is parsimonious while spanning the full range of practically meaningful policies.
