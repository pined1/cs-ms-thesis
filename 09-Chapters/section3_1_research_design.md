# Section 3.1: Research Design and Approach

This study investigates whether the scope of incident knowledge sharing causally affects organizational reliability — specifically, whether teams that share what they learn from software incidents beyond their own boundaries produce fewer recurrent failures than teams that retain that knowledge locally. Answering this question rigorously requires more than observing a correlation between sharing practices and reliability outcomes. It requires controlled manipulation: holding the structure of the organization, the frequency of incidents, and the cognitive capacity of agents constant while varying only the scope of knowledge sharing. Field studies cannot do this — organizations differ in too many confounding ways for causal attribution to be clean. Surveys can document what practitioners believe about sharing practices, and regression analyses can confirm that sharing scope correlates with reliability metrics, but neither approach can rule out alternative explanations. Only simulation provides the experimental control necessary to isolate the mechanism of interest and make a causal claim about how sharing scope produces — or fails to produce — organizational reliability.

Among simulation methods, agent-based modeling (ABM) is specifically suited to this research problem because organizational reliability is an emergent phenomenon. It does not reside in any single decision, policy, or team; it arises from thousands of small, distributed learning events — an on-call engineer updates a runbook, a post-mortem finding propagates to a neighboring team, a pattern recognized in one system shapes how an alert is interpreted in another — unfolding across twenty teams over a simulated year of 365 days. Bonabeau (2002) argues that ABMs are well-suited precisely to domains where macro-level behavior emerges from local micro-level interactions that cannot be adequately captured by aggregate equations. Harrison et al. (2007) demonstrate this logic in organizational and management research, showing that ABM enables researchers to examine how individual-level rules and interactions give rise to firm-level outcomes that would otherwise appear to be the product of top-down design. This study follows that tradition: each simulated agent represents a team with bounded memory, a local incident history, and rules governing when and with whom learning is shared. Organizational reliability is not assumed — it is grown from those interactions.

The choice of ABM over purely predictive or statistical approaches reflects a commitment to generative explanation. Epstein (1999) articulates the foundational claim: "If you didn't grow it, you didn't explain its emergence." A regression study might show that organizations with broader sharing scope experience fewer recurring incidents — but it would not show how that relationship is produced. The simulation in this study does not claim to predict exactly how many incidents a particular organization will experience in a given quarter. That would require calibration to a specific organization's history, team structure, and incident taxonomy. Instead, this study aims to explain the mechanism: the process by which knowledge, when shared across team boundaries, reduces the probability that a failure pattern already witnessed in one part of the system goes unrecognized in another. The analogy to plate tectonics is instructive — the theory explains why earthquakes occur and where they are likely without predicting the next one. Mechanism and prediction are distinct epistemic goals, and ABM serves the former.

Because simulation results are only as credible as the assumptions that produce them, this study makes a concurrent commitment to rigorous validation. A model that generates plausible-looking outputs for arbitrary reasons provides no explanatory leverage. The verification and validation framework developed by Sargent (2020) guides this study's approach to confirming that the simulation correctly implements its intended design and that the model's behavior corresponds meaningfully to the real-world phenomenon it represents. The full validation approach — including face validity assessments, sensitivity analysis, and boundary condition tests — is detailed in Section 3.8. The remainder of Chapter 3 describes the specific design decisions that operationalize this research approach: the simulation architecture, agent knowledge representation, incident generation and propagation logic, experimental conditions, and data collection procedures.

## Committee Watch

**What they will scrutinize:**

1. **"Causal" language without randomization.** The opening paragraph claims this study can make causal claims via simulation. The committee — especially a methodologist — may push back: simulation does not provide causal identification in the econometric sense. Be prepared to clarify: the claim is *internal* causality within the model, not external causal inference about real organizations. The simulation establishes that in this model, varying sharing scope while holding everything else constant produces reliability differences. Whether that mechanism operates in real organizations is a separate (and explicitly limited) question addressed in Section 5.X (limitations).

2. **Generativity vs. validity.** The Epstein (1999) generative argument is compelling but can invite the question: "You grew it — but did you grow the *right* thing?" The answer lives in Section 3.8 (validation). Be ready to point there immediately if this comes up and explain that face validity, sensitivity analysis, and boundary tests are the answer.

3. **Why not system dynamics (SD)?** SD is another simulation method for organizational phenomena. The committee may ask why ABM was chosen over SD. The answer: SD models aggregate flows and stocks; it cannot represent individual team memory, heterogeneous learning rates, or the specific topology of who shares with whom. The mechanism of interest is inherently agent-level.

4. **The plate tectonics analogy.** This is rhetorically effective but some committee members may find it imprecise. If challenged, clarify that the analogy is limited to the explanation-vs.-prediction distinction and is not meant to imply that organizational learning obeys physical laws.
