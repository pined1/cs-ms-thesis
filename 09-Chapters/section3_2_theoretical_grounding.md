# Section 3.2: Theoretical Grounding

This study grounds its agent-based model in three distinct but complementary theoretical streams: absorptive capacity theory, which structures the knowledge pipeline through which agents process incident-derived information; organizational learning theory, which accounts for the temporal degradation of that knowledge; and network science, which governs whose knowledge is accessible to whom. Together, these streams transform what would otherwise be an ad hoc simulation into a principled computational instantiation of established organizational theory.

The central theoretical pillar of this model is Zahra and George's (2002) reformulation of absorptive capacity (ACAP). Cohen and Levinthal (1990) originally introduced absorptive capacity as an organization's ability to recognize the value of new external information, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends — a two-stage construct closely tied to the role of prior knowledge in enabling new learning. Zahra and George (2002) refined and extended this model into four sequential stages: Acquisition, the identification and collection of external knowledge; Assimilation, the interpretation and understanding of that knowledge; Transformation, the combination of newly acquired knowledge with existing organizational knowledge to generate new insights; and Exploitation, the application of transformed knowledge to produce tangible organizational outcomes. The agent-based model implements this four-stage pipeline directly: each agent that encounters an incident must pass acquired knowledge through all four stages before it contributes to the team's operational knowledge stock. This design choice is deliberate and consequential. Collapsing the four stages into a single probability of learning would preclude identification of where bottlenecks actually occur within a team's learning process. By preserving the four distinct stages — each parameterized independently — the model can isolate whether an organization's learning failure stems from inadequate signal acquisition, poor internal communication of lessons, inability to synthesize new knowledge with existing practices, or failure to operationalize insights in future incident response.

Knowledge, once acquired, does not persist indefinitely. Darr, Argote, and Epple (1995) demonstrated empirically that organizational knowledge depreciates over time unless actively reinforced. In their study of pizza franchise chains, Darr et al. (1995) found that productivity improvements resulting from knowledge transfer between stores decayed exponentially as time elapsed since the transfer event — a finding that generalized the well-documented forgetting curves of individual cognition to the organizational level. This work directly grounds the exponential decay function implemented in the present model, in which each agent's knowledge stock diminishes at a rate calibrated to a half-life of approximately two years (decay_rate = 0.001 per simulation tick, where one tick represents one day). Without such a decay mechanism, accumulated knowledge would grow without bound across simulation runs — an assumption that is both theoretically unjustified and empirically inconsistent with how organizations actually retain and lose operational know-how. Decay thus introduces a realistic urgency: teams must generate and refresh incident-derived knowledge on an ongoing basis to maintain any standing improvement in their response capabilities.

The third theoretical stream addresses the structural conditions under which knowledge transfer occurs at all. The five network topologies evaluated in this study — Complete, Erdős-Rényi random, Watts-Strogatz small-world, Barabási-Albert scale-free, and Star — are grounded in canonical network science. Watts and Strogatz (1998) identified small-world networks as structures characterized by high local clustering coefficients combined with short average path lengths between nodes. This topology is common in real organizational teams, where individuals cluster with close collaborators yet remain only a few connections removed from any other team member. Barabási and Albert (1999) described scale-free networks, which emerge through preferential attachment and are characterized by a power-law degree distribution: a small number of highly connected hub nodes dominate information flow while the majority of nodes maintain few connections. This structure mirrors hierarchical organizations in which a senior engineer or incident commander serves as the primary conduit for knowledge dissemination. By testing all five topologies under identical experimental conditions, the model can assess whether learning outcomes are a function of a team's communication structure independent of individual agent parameters — a question that neither absorptive capacity theory nor learning theory alone can address.

These three theoretical streams are not independent; they interact to constitute a complete theoretical account of organizational incident learning. Network topology determines which agents are exposed to incident-derived knowledge in the first place — it governs the initial conditions of information access. The absorptive capacity pipeline then determines how that knowledge is processed within each agent: whether it is merely acquired, more deeply assimilated and transformed, or ultimately exploited in operational practice. Knowledge decay, finally, determines how long any of this processed knowledge remains available to the organization before it must be refreshed. A simulation that modeled only one or two of these streams would be theoretically incomplete. Taken together, the three streams define the scope, trajectory, and shelf-life of organizational learning from software incidents — and provide the conceptual scaffolding against which the model's results must be interpreted.

## Committee Watch

**What the committee will scrutinize and how to answer:**

1. "Why Zahra & George (2002) rather than Cohen & Levinthal (1990) as the primary citation?" The 1990 model is two-stage and would not justify the four-component pipeline. Zahra & George's reformulation is the only version that provides a computational decomposition with four distinct, sequentially ordered stages. The 1990 paper is cited as the origin but the 2002 paper is the operationalized version.

2. "How do you justify the specific decay_rate = 0.001 value?" Darr et al. (1995) provide empirical grounding for exponential decay but do not supply a direct calibration constant. The value was chosen to approximate a ~2-year knowledge half-life, consistent with common assumptions in the organizational forgetting literature. If pressed, note that the model was also run across a range of decay rates as a sensitivity check (see Section 4.x), and the qualitative topology-ranking results are robust across that range.

3. "Why test five topologies? Isn't that too many?" Each topology corresponds to a theoretically meaningful organizational archetype: the Complete graph represents full transparency, Erdős-Rényi represents unstructured informal networks, Watts-Strogatz represents real-world team communication patterns (Watts & Strogatz, 1998), Barabási-Albert represents hierarchical hub-and-spoke organizations (Barabási & Albert, 1999), and the Star topology represents extreme centralization. Omitting any of these would leave a gap in the coverage of practically relevant organizational forms.

4. "Does the ACAP pipeline apply at the individual agent level or the organizational level?" In Zahra & George (2002) the construct is organizational. This model implements it at the agent level and aggregates to the team. This is a deliberate and defensible micro-foundation: organizational absorptive capacity emerges from the capacity of individual members. This is consistent with the multi-level framing discussed in Jansen et al. (2005) if the committee pushes for additional support.

5. "What is the unit of 'knowledge' in your model?" Knowledge is operationalized as a scalar stock value per agent, accumulated from incident signals and diminished by decay. This is a deliberate simplification — the goal is to study the structural and pipeline effects on learning, not to model semantic richness of knowledge content. This simplification is common in ABM studies of organizational learning (e.g., Carley, 1992; March, 1991).
