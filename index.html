Organizational Learning from Software Incidents

An agent-based simulation study of how incident knowledge sharing scope affects organizational reliability in software engineering teams.

1. Introduction & Study Overview

1.1 The Central Problem

At scale, software systems fail. Outages, database failures, also failed deployments are not anomolies in modern engineering organizations, they are the operational baseline.

High-reliability organizations are distinguished not by whether incidents occur—they do—but by how quickly they move from incident to structural improvement, reducing the base incident rate for those failure modes.

Most engineering organizations hold postmortems: structured retrospectives where teams document what went wrong, why it went wrong, and what they will change to prevent recurrence. A more nuanced and less understood question is whether the breadth of postmortem knowledge distribution actually impacts organizational outcomes.

In practice, software engineering organizations like Google have formalized postmortem culture as a mechanism for learning from production failures. Most teams hold these structured reviews; the open question is not whether to conduct them, but how to distribute the insights they generate.

If Team A experiences a database failure and writes a detailed postmortem, should only Team A read it? Should Team A's direct neighbors in the organization also read it? Should the entire company? Broader sharing intuitively seems better, but there is a real cost: meeting time, cognitive load, alert fatigue, and the risk of overwhelming teams with incidents that feel irrelevant to them. The tradeoff is real, and no rigorous simulation study has examined how different sharing strategies actually affect organizational reliability outcomes. This thesis addresses that gap.

1.2 What We Study: The 4-Stage Pipeline

The backbone of the learning model comes from Zahra & George's (2002) reformulation of absorptive capacity theory — the idea that organizations do not simply "receive" knowledge from outside; they must acquire it, understand it, connect it to existing knowledge, and apply it operationally before behavior changes. Rather than treating learning as a single binary event, the model implements a four-stage pipeline through which incident knowledge must pass before it changes a team's behavior.

The four stages are: Acquisition (did the team receive the postmortem?), Assimilation (did the team understand it?), Transformation (did the team connect it to what they already know?), and Exploitation (did the team change behavior to reduce future incidents?). Each stage has an independent probability of success, and a team must pass all four to convert an incident into reduced organizational risk.

Stage 1 — Acquisition: Does the team receive the incident knowledge? A team that is not in the sharing scope for an incident never even sees the postmortem. Teams within scope receive the incident with a probability that reflects their current communication network. Acquisition is the moment a team reads a postmortem and registers that it exists and is relevant to them.

Stage 2 — Assimilation: Does the team that has passed the acquisition stage understand the knowledge? Even when a team receives a postmortem, they may not fully grasp its implications for their own systems. Assimilation probability is influenced by the team's existing knowledge base.

Stage 3 — Transformation: Does the team connect the new knowledge to what they already know? Transformation is the cognitive work of integration. This stage has the highest failure point in the model, reflecting real-world evidence that cross-domain knowledge transfer is difficult.

Stage 4 — Exploitation: Does the team actually change its behavior to reduce future incidents? This is the stage at which reliability outcomes are measured. A team that fully exploits an incident's lesson updates its runbooks, monitoring, and configurations—changes that reduce how often that class of failure occurs.

Transformation vs. Exploitation — Concrete Distinction
Stage Concrete Example
Transformation A team reads a database postmortem and realizes their own connection pool configuration has the same vulnerability — they mentally connect it to their system. Cognitive connection made, structural understanding updated; no action taken yet.
Exploitation The team actually changes the connection pool configuration, updates the runbook, and adds a monitoring alert — behavior changes, and incident risk drops.

Stage 4 is the end of the pipeline — but completing it once does not mean the organization is done learning. Each new incident restarts the process. The cumulative probability of passing all four stages determines a team's learning velocity — how quickly the organization converts incident experience into reduced risk. A team with high acquisition probability but low transformation probability acquires many postmortems but converts few into structural improvements. An organization's overall learning velocity is the product of all four stage rates, which is why sharing scope (which determines who even enters Stage 1) dominates the other parameters: a team that never acquires a postmortem cannot benefit from high assimilation or exploitation rates.

1.3 The Agent-Based Model

The model is reflective of an organization, with twenty software engineering teams arranged in a social network — some teams are adjacent, some are distant in their communication. Each team owns a subsystem of the software system, and those subsystems generate incidents over time according to a stochastic process every day. This study uses 20 teams as the base configuration; the model supports arbitrary team counts.

An analogy: a technology company with 20 engineering teams, each team owns a set of software services and those services experience outages. After each outage, the team writes a postmortem. Now, the question is: should the postmortem findings stay just within the team that experienced the outage, be shared with adjacent teams from their communication network, or broadcast across the entire engineering organization? Our model simulates exactly this decision, run 100 times over a simulated year, under four different sharing strategies.

The simulation was designed as an agent-based model (ABM) — a computational approach in which individual agents (in this case, teams) follow defined rules, and their collective behavior produces emergent organizational outcomes that cannot be predicted from the rules alone. ABMs are well suited to this problem because they allow for the kinds of complex, unplanned patterns that arise in real organizations, where no single authority controls how knowledge flows.

Four sharing strategies are tested: NONE, LOCAL, NEIGHBOR, and GLOBAL. These conditions are defined formally in Section 1.4. For now, the key idea is that teams can choose how widely they share incident postmortems — from keeping knowledge entirely within the team that experienced the failure, to broadcasting it across the entire organization.

One key mechanism governs how knowledge travels under NEIGHBOR conditions: signal decay. Each hop an incident postmortem travels through the network reduces the acquisition probability of the receiving team:

p_acquire = acquisition_prob × signal_decay ^ path_length

In plain terms: The further away a team sits from the incident source in the communication network, the less likely it is to successfully pick up the postmortem — a 20% drop per hop means a team two hops away starts at only 57.6% acquisition probability even if the base rate is 90%.

Under GLOBAL sharing, every team is treated as path_length = 1 regardless of their actual network position — the signal does not decay with distance. Under NEIGHBOR sharing, only teams that are direct connections (path_length = 1) receive the postmortem at all, and the decay formula applies to those transmissions. Signal decay is introduced here because it is a structural property of the NEIGHBOR strategy; its effect on organizational outcomes is examined directly in H4.

What a Team Actually "Knows" — The Knowledge Vector

Every team in the simulation carries a knowledge vector — a 5×3 grid of 15 values, one for each combination of incident type and knowledge dimension. This is the team's organizational memory: what it has learned from past incidents, either directly or through shared postmortems. Each team maintains a separate knowledge score for every subsystem in the organization — not just their own. This means a team can have strong knowledge about incidents in another team's subsystem if they have received and processed enough postmortems from that team.

Each team's Prevention Knowledge vector tracks understanding of the specific incident types that have occurred in the organization. Because different teams own different subsystems with different failure modes, teams enter the simulation with different baseline knowledge configurations — Team A's experience with database timeouts does not directly transfer to Team B's experience with network partitions without passing through the transformation stage.

1.4 The Four Sharing Strategies

These four conditions are the independent variable of this study. Every experiment in this report runs the simulation once under each condition. The four conditions are defined formally below. These definitions apply consistently across all subsequent sections.

  • NONE — No team reads or learns from the incident postmortem, including the team that experienced it. This is the true baseline: incidents occur and are logged, but no organizational learning happens.
  • LOCAL — The source team writes and reads the postmortem, gains knowledge, and improves its practices. No other team receives it.

1.5 Why This Question Matters: Literature Context

Despite industry-wide investment in postmortem processes, the literature on how sharing scope affects reliability outcomes has not been widely discussed. Organizational learning theory [@march1991; @argote2011] holds that knowledge must flow through an organization to be useful. Absorptive capacity theory (Cohen & Levinthal 1990; Zahra & George 2002) explains that receiving knowledge is not enough — teams must also have the capacity to absorb and apply it. These theories have not been tested in the specific context of software incident knowledge sharing with a formal computational model. This simulation study provides that test, bridging organizational learning theory and absorptive capacity theory in a setting grounded in real engineering practice.

Hypothesis Factor Under Test Predicted Outcome
H1 — Sharing Scope How widely incident knowledge is shared after a failure GLOBAL sharing produces the fewest incidents; NONE produces the most. The expected ordering: NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL.
H2 — Deployment Velocity How frequently teams ship code to production The faster the deployment rate, the higher the incident rate — more deployments create more opportunities for failure.
Note on terminology — Exploitation (Stage 4) is the stage where a team translates accumulated knowledge into actual system changes: runbook updates, configuration fixes, and monitoring improvements. The parameter P_exploit controls the probability that a team successfully completes this stage on a given timestep.
H3 — Exploitation Effectiveness How intensely teams act on the knowledge they hold Early improvements in how teams use knowledge produce the biggest gains. As effectiveness increases, each additional unit of effort saves fewer incidents — diminishing returns.
H4 — Network Density How many connections exist between teams The more interconnected the organization, the faster knowledge spreads and the fewer total incidents occur.

A Note on H3 — Why Diminishing Returns?

We expect diminishing returns based on how absorptive capacity works in theory. At low exploitation effectiveness, even modest improvements in how teams act on their knowledge should prevent many incidents — because the easiest, most common failure modes are still unaddressed. As effectiveness increases, those readily-preventable failures are already being caught, and each additional unit of effort must address increasingly rare or complex failure modes. In software reliability terms: moving a team from zero learning to some learning should prevent far more incidents than moving them from good to excellent, because the high-frequency easy wins are gone and the remaining risk is harder to eliminate. Whether this concave relationship actually appears in the simulation data is what H3 tests.

1.7 Preview of Key Findings

Key Finding: Global incident knowledge sharing reduces organizational incidents by 45% compared to no sharing. This effect holds across all robustness tests (Cohen's d = 11.51, p < 0.001). All four hypotheses received support from the data.

A Cohen's d of 11.51 is extraordinarily large—roughly five times larger than the standard for "very large" effects. With p < 0.001, this result is essentially certain, not due to chance. In plain terms: there is zero overlap between the outcomes—every GLOBAL run beats every NONE run.

2.1 Introduction

Does sharing incident knowledge more broadly reduce system failures? H1 states: GLOBAL > NEIGHBOR > LOCAL > NONE, where "greater" means fewer incidents and higher availability. This section covers the evidence for that ordering and examines how robust it is across a wide range of conditions.

The expected ordering is NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL in total incidents. This inverse relationship—more incidents paired with less accumulated knowledge—should hold if the model is correct. If any reversal appeared in these results, it would indicate a model implementation error. Confirming this ordering is the primary purpose of this experiment.

In many software organizations, when a production outage occurs, the on-call team investigates, resolves the issue, and documents findings in a postmortem [cite cook1998 or lunney2016]. That document contains critical lessons: which configuration was wrong, what monitoring was missing, how the failure cascaded. The question is what happens to those lessons once the incident is over.

If the report stays in the owning team's private Confluence page, that is LOCAL learning — knowledge isolated to the team that lived through the failure. If the team posts it in a shared Slack channel that adjacent teams follow, that is NEIGHBOR-level learning. If the team presents findings in a company-wide incident review attended by all engineering teams, that is GLOBAL learning — every team hears about it and can update their own practices. The simulation follows this same logic and tests which scope produces the fewest repeated failures.

3.1 Introduction

Does deploying software updates more frequently cause more incidents? H2 investigates this tension directly. H2 tests whether incident counts rise with deployment frequency, and whether this effect depends on whether knowledge spreads globally or stays local. H2 is confirmed if higher deployment rates produce more incidents, and if this effect is smaller under GLOBAL sharing than under LOCAL. Without learning effects, we would expect incidents to rise proportionally with deployment rate. A flat curve under GLOBAL sharing would instead reveal that knowledge accumulation is suppressing deployment risk.

The intuition is straightforward: more deployments mean more chances to introduce an incident into production. Each change event carries an elevated probability that something breaks. If learning does not keep pace with the rate of change, incidents accumulate linearly. The key question is whether knowledge accumulation can blunt that trajectory. Does the answer depend on how broadly lessons are shared?

Consider two software engineering teams deploying bug fixes and feature updates to production regularly. Team A deploys once a week; Team B deploys every day or two. In the short run, Team B will encounter more incidents simply because they are pushing changes more often. Now suppose Team B also learns faster: every outage becomes a learning opportunity, the team writes a postmortem, and those lessons may either stay local or spread to the broader organization. If incident knowledge is shared globally, Prevention K (the team's accumulated incident-prevention knowledge, a value from 0 to 1) rises across all teams, and future deployments carry less marginal risk (the additional incident probability added by each new deployment). If knowledge stays local, most teams have not learned from their peers' failures, and the deployment multiplier continues to act on a high baseline. The same number of deployments can produce very different reliability outcomes depending on how learning is structured.

Intuition: Going from 0.05 to 0.50 represents a 10× increase in deployment frequency. Yet GLOBAL incidents rise only from 152.5 to 188.4, showing a near-flat GLOBAL curve. When an organization has been operating under GLOBAL learning for a sustained period, Prevention K (the team's accumulated incident-prevention knowledge, a value from 0 to 1) approaches 0.992. 3.5 The Cross-Sweep: H2 × H3 Interaction (Experiment 10)

Experiment 10 crossed three deployment rates (0.05, 0.10, 0.30) with three exploitation probabilities (0.2, 0.6, 0.9) under GLOBAL sharing with 100 seeds per cell. The question: does investing more effort in postmortems change the deployment-incident relationship? The result: no. Deployment rate drives incidents; exploitation effort does not interact with it — each lever acts independently.

Experiment 10 asks whether deployment velocity and exploitation intensity interact — in other words, does deploying faster make learning more or less valuable? To isolate this, the simulation held all other parameters fixed and swept both deployment rate and prevention_effect simultaneously across a grid of combinations.

H2 establishes that deployment velocity drives incidents upward. H3 (covered in the next section) asks whether investing more effort in postmortems — higher exploitation probability — reduces incidents. Before analyzing each hypothesis in isolation, it is worth asking whether these two levers interact: does the benefit of working harder on postmortems grow larger or smaller depending on how fast a team is shipping?

Experiment 10 was run specifically to answer this question. It crossed three deployment rate values (0.05, 0.10, 0.30) with three exploitation probability values (0.2, 0.6, 0.9), producing a 3 × 3 grid of conditions run under the GLOBAL sharing scenario, 100 seeds each. 5.1 Introduction

Does the shape of how teams are connected affect how well incident knowledge spreads? H4 investigates whether network structure is a meaningful lever for organizational reliability. All four topologies use the same 20 teams — only the pattern of connections between them changes.

5.2 Key Terms: Understanding Network Topology

Before reading the results, these terms are used throughout this section:

  • Topology — the pattern of connections between teams. Two organizations can have the same 20 teams but very different topologies depending on how those teams communicate.
  • Hub team — a team with many connections to other teams. They receive postmortems from many sources and can spread knowledge widely.
  • Peripheral team — a team with few connections, typically on the edge of the network. They receive fewer postmortems and may be isolated from knowledge that could help them.
  • Hub-spoke network — a topology where a small number of hub teams connect to many peripheral teams, but peripheral teams have few connections to each other. Think of an airport hub: all flights route through Chicago, few go directly city-to-city.
  • Betweenness centrality — a measure of how often a node lies on the shortest path between two other nodes. A team with high betweenness is a "bridge" — remove them and many knowledge paths break.

5.3 The Formula: How Network Position Affects Learning

Under NEIGHBOR sharing, a team's probability of receiving a postmortem decreases with distance from the source:

P_{\text{acquire}} = P_{\text{base}} \times \delta^{d}

What each term means: - P_base — the base acquisition probability for a direct neighbor (teams directly connected receive postmortems with this probability) - δ (delta) — the signal decay factor, a value between 0 and 1. Each additional hop reduces the probability by this factor. - d — the number of network hops (edges) between the source team and the receiving team

In plain terms: If a team is 2 hops away from the incident source, they receive the postmortem with probability P_base × δ². The farther away, the less likely they ever see it at all.

Note on signal decay: Signal decay only matters under NEIGHBOR sharing, where knowledge travels hop-by-hop through the network. Under GLOBAL sharing, every team receives every postmortem regardless of distance — signal decay has no effect.

Does signal decay matter under NEIGHBOR sharing? Since NEIGHBOR already restricts sharing to direct connections (d = 1), signal decay multiplies against d = 1 only. This means the decay formula reduces to: P_base × δ¹ = 0.9 × 0.8 = 0.72. Signal decay still matters here: even direct neighbors have a 72% (not 100%) acquisition chance. The key topology effect is not multi-hop decay but rather which teams qualify as neighbors at all. In a star topology, when a spoke team has an incident, only the hub and zero other spokes are neighbors — acquisition is limited to one team regardless of decay. In a complete graph, all 19 other teams are neighbors — all 19 have a 72% acquisition chance. Topology determines the pool; signal decay determines the per-team probability within that pool.

The key insight: denser, more distributed topologies minimize average path length (and maximize neighborhood size under NEIGHBOR sharing), which maximizes Stage 1 acquisition probability across the organization.

An important clarification: NEIGHBOR scope and signal decay are distinct mechanisms.

Under NEIGHBOR sharing, knowledge only travels one hop — directly adjacent teams. Signal decay applies at that one hop, reducing base acquisition probability from 90% to 72%. Teams two or more hops away receive nothing under NEIGHBOR regardless of signal decay — they are simply outside the sharing scope entirely. The decay formula does not reach them because the postmortem is never transmitted that far.

This is why topology matters so much under NEIGHBOR: the distance between teams determines who receives any knowledge at all. In a star topology, peripheral teams are 2 hops from each other through the hub — meaning they receive nothing from each other's incidents. In a complete or small-world topology, most teams are 1 hop apart, so knowledge circulates broadly. The signal decay rate (0.8) modulates the probability of that one-hop transmission; the topology determines whether a transmission path exists at all.

6. Ablation Tests — exp11 (Knowledge Decay) & exp12 (Source Asymmetry)

An ablation test removes one component from a model to see whether the model's core finding still holds without it. If the finding survives intact, that component wasn't essential to the result; if it collapses, the component is load-bearing. This section runs two ablations on the H1 model — removing a knowledge-decay function and a source-team routing advantage — to test whether the H1 ordering (NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL) emerges from the sharing-scope mechanism alone or depends on these auxiliary design choices. 6.1 What Is an Ablation Test?

Why We Run Ablations. In agent-based models, a component's importance is revealed by removing it. If the model's main result survives removal, that component wasn't essential; if the result collapses, the component was critical. This is the standard test for distinguishing genuine architectural properties from tuning artifacts (Sargent 2020).

What We're Removing. We target two components in H1: the knowledge-decay function (exp11) and the source-team learning shortcut (exp12). Both were designed to make the model more realistic, but their necessity to the H1 result is unknown until tested.

What We're Checking. For each ablation, we ask: does the H1 ordering — NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL — persist? If yes, the ordering is a property of the sharing-scope architecture itself. If no, the removed component was load-bearing.

6.2 exp11 — Removing Knowledge Decay

6.2.1 Background: Knowledge Decay in the Agent Model

Each agent in this simulation maintains a Prevention Knowledge vector K, a real-valued score (bounded [0, 1]) that represents how well that agent's team currently understands how to prevent a given class of software incidents. Prevention Knowledge is the primary mechanism through which learning reduces incident rates: higher K values lower the probability that the agent's team causes a future incident of that type.

Knowledge decay was introduced to capture organizational memory loss over time — the empirical reality that teams forget procedures, lose institutional knowledge through staff changes, and allow process discipline to fade when incidents are infrequent. In the model this is implemented as follows: Every single day, each team loses a small percentage (0.1%) of its accumulated knowledge about preventing failures—whether an incident happened that day or not. This represents how prevention practices fade unless actively reinforced.

6.2.2 Formal Agent State-Update Rule

The decay component of the agent state update is:

K(t)  =  K(t-1) × (1 − decay_rate)
VariableMeaning in this model
K(t)The agent's Prevention Knowledge score at the end of timestep t (one simulated day). Each team tracks knowledge about different kinds of failures (like database crashes, timeout errors, deployment issues). Each type gets its own knowledge score, and every score decays each day independently. If your team knows 80% how to prevent database crashes, tomorrow it knows 79.92%.
K(t-1)The same agent's Prevention Knowledge score at the previous timestep — the value before today's decay (and any learning events) are applied.
decay_rateDaily fractional fading of accumulated knowledge. Default value: 0.001 per timestep. Models staff turnover, process atrophy, and the slow fading of practices that are not continually reinforced. Applied unconditionally every day — it does not wait for incidents to occur.
(1 − decay_rate)Retention factor per day: 0.999. Over 365 days compounded, a unit of knowledge retained is 0.999365 ≈ 0.694 — meaning roughly 31% of knowledge earned at the start of the year fades by year-end if never replenished.

In plain terms: Every single day, each team quietly loses a small fraction (0.1%) of its accumulated incident knowledge, regardless of whether any incident occurred — representing the real-world reality that prevention practices fade unless actively reinforced, and whether this slow drain materially affects outcomes depends on how fast sharing-driven learning can outpace it.

This is applied independently to every element of every agent's K vector, every timestep, regardless of whether a learning event occurred on that day. The rule is not activated by incidents; it runs unconditionally.

Numerical example. Consider a unit of knowledge earned on day 1. After 365 daily applications of the decay rule, its retained fraction is:

  (1 − 0.001)^365  =  0.999^365  ≈  0.694

  → A 30.6% fading of knowledge earned at the start of the year,
    if that knowledge is never replenished.

The magnitude is intentionally modest — decay does not erase knowledge rapidly — but it is persistent and cumulative. Whether decay has a measurable effect on outcomes depends on whether learning events replenish knowledge fast enough to offset the constant drain.

6.2.3 Ablation Design

To test whether knowledge fading actually matters, we ran the simulation again but turned off the daily fade. If the fade was important, removing it would improve results. If it wasn't important, results would stay the same. We changed exactly one thing: decay_rate was set to 0.0, meaning knowledge once learned is never forgotten. Everything else in the simulation stayed identical to the H1 setup.

6.2.4 Ablation Isolation Check: the NONE Scenario

In the no-learning baseline (NONE), teams never accumulate knowledge in the first place, so there's nothing to fade. The fade mechanism should have zero effect on NONE's outcome. Think of it like this: if you never save money, whether or not your savings account slowly loses value is irrelevant. If NONE produced different results between the two versions, it would mean we broke something in our code. It didn't — we got 484.3 incidents in both cases — confirming our test is clean and the code works correctly.

Bottom line: The architecture of how knowledge spreads (which teams learn from which incidents) matters far more than the rate at which knowledge fades. Smart sharing networks that include many teams are more powerful than perfect memory.

Finding — Exp 11: Knowledge Decay Is a Secondary Factor

Removing knowledge decay (setting decay_rate = 0.0) produces measurable but modest improvements across all learning scenarios, with the largest absolute gain in NEIGHBOR (−13.0 incidents, −3.9%). The differential impact across scenarios is mechanistically explained by accumulation dynamics: decay fades most efficiently the moderate, slowly accumulating knowledge characteristic of hop-limited propagation, while rapid replenishment under GLOBAL effectively neutralizes decay before it compounds.

The NONE scenario's zero change (484.3 in both conditions) confirms the ablation is correctly isolated: decay has no effect when no knowledge is ever accumulated, validating that the parameter change introduced no confounding side-effects.

Overall verdict: Knowledge decay is a real but secondary factor. The primary driver of incident reduction in this model is learning breadth (which scenarios learn, and how many teams receive each update) — not the rate at which that knowledge is subsequently lost. Investing in broader knowledge propagation yields larger returns than investing in mechanisms to slow decay.

6.3 exp12 — Removing Source Asymmetry

6.3.1 What Source Asymmetry Is and Why It Exists

Every incident in the simulation is owned by exactly one team — the source team — which manages the subsystem that failed. When a post-incident learning cycle begins, the model must decide how to route the source team through the four-stage knowledge pipeline. The four stages are:

  1. Stage 1 — Acquisition (P ≈ 0.9 at zero hops): the team reads and registers the postmortem.
  2. Stage 2 — Assimilation (P = 0.70): the team deeply processes what the postmortem says.
  3. Stage 3 — Transformation (cosine-similarity gate): the team checks whether the postmortem's knowledge vector aligns sufficiently with its own subsystem's knowledge representation. Only postmortems whose content is recognizably relevant to a team's domain pass this gate.
  4. Stage 4 — Exploitation (P_exploit = 0.60): the team implements a concrete prevention action, updating its Prevention Knowledge vector.

The source asymmetry rule is a deliberate routing shortcut for the source team only. The rationale: the source team lived through the incident in real time. Routing them through a pipeline designed to help remote teams understand a document they were not present for would misrepresent their learning — they already hold direct, first-hand knowledge of what happened and why. Accordingly, in the default model, the source team is skipped from Stages 2–4 entirely and receives a direct, 100%-effective update to its Prevention Knowledge vector. This is called an implicit update: certain, immediate, and not contingent on any probabilistic gate.

The near-zero change (~0 incidents) is precisely the redundancy signal: GLOBAL's strength is that no single agent is a bottleneck, and this ablation confirms it.

Under GLOBAL, the source team is 1 of 20 learners; the same degradation of that one channel is absorbed by the 19 redundant learners and produces essentially no change (~0). The asymmetric response across scenarios is itself evidence for the H1 mechanism: it is information exposure breadth — not any single agent's learning efficiency — that drives the ordering. The H1 ordering (NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL) is preserved under both conditions, confirming that the H1 result is not an artifact of the source asymmetry design choice.

6.4 Combined Conclusion: What exp11 and exp12 Together Establish

The H1 ordering — NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL in cumulative incidents — is a structural property of the knowledge-sharing architecture, not an artifact of the specific modeling choices for knowledge decay or source-based routing asymmetry. This ordering emerged in the baseline experiments and survives the removal of both components.

The two ablations establish this in three ways:

  1. The H1 ordering is invariant across both ablations. Exp11 (no decay) and exp12 (no asymmetry) each simplify the model in a different dimension, yet the rank order NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL is preserved in both cases. Because decay and asymmetry are independent mechanisms — one governs retention over time, the other governs which knowledge sources agents prefer — their joint inability to disrupt the ordering establishes it as a consequence of the knowledge-flow topology itself, not of any particular parameterization choice.

  2. When components are removed, outcomes shift in the direction and magnitude the model's own logic predicts. Removing decay (exp11) reduces incident counts modestly and uniformly across conditions, consistent with the prediction that agents retain competence longer and therefore make fewer errors. Removing asymmetry (exp12) narrows inter-condition gaps in a direction consistent with the prediction that source-routing advantages are eliminated. Neither ablation produces a surprise reversal or a null result where a strong effect was expected. This directional consistency is a model validity signal: the model behaves as its causal logic requires, not just as its parameters are tuned to produce.

  3. Exp12 specifically identifies the mechanism behind LOCAL's performance ceiling. In the baseline, LOCAL consistently underperforms NONE — a counterintuitive result, since sharing knowledge within a team should reduce incidents. Removing asymmetry narrows LOCAL's disadvantage relative to NONE more than it affects any other condition. This implicates LOCAL's reliance on a single high-certainty learning channel: agents in LOCAL preferentially absorb from their most confident teammate, which concentrates knowledge in one path and leaves gaps that decay (in the baseline) then exploits. Exp12 reveals this not as a generic sharing-is-risky result, but as a consequence of the asymmetric routing rule that defines how LOCAL agents select their learning source.

Together, these two ablations establish that the model's core behavioral claims are load-bearing properties of its architectural choices rather than tuning artifacts, which means the next productive step is calibrating agent-level parameters to empirical incident data from real software organizations — the model's structure is stable enough to support that comparison.

More sharing produces more learning events, increasing learning overhead. It also builds more prevention knowledge, reducing future incidents and the firefighting hours they consume. learning_cost = 2.0 hours per successful learning event (per team per incident)

Each simulation tick (simplified):
  1. Generate incidents (stochastic, per-subsystem)
  2. Trigger post-incident learning pipeline (Stages 1–4, unchanged from H1)
  3. Update agent Prevention Knowledge vectors (unchanged from H1)
  4. Record cost: firefighting_cost += incident_cost; learning_cost += event_cost  ← measurement only

8. Sensitivity Sweeps — Which Model Knobs Matter?

This section probes the robustness of the H1 finding by varying individual model parameters across wide ranges and asking whether the qualitative result (NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL) and its quantitative magnitude survive recalibration. Before presenting any individual sweep, the methodology subsections below describe what was held constant and what was allowed to vary.

8.1 Methodology: How Sensitivity Sweeps Work

A sensitivity sweep tests whether a model's main finding holds when one parameter is varied across a range while everything else stays constant. If the finding holds across a wide range, the model is robust to that parameter. If the finding flips or vanishes within the range, the model is sensitive to that parameter — and the value chosen for the experiment matters a lot.

Sensitivity sweeps differ from ablations: an ablation removes a component entirely; a sweep varies a component's value across its range. Together, they answer: "Is this finding real, or is it an artifact of specific parameter choices?"

8.2 Baseline H1 Setup (What Stayed the Same)

For every sweep in this section, the following H1 conditions were held constant:

  • Watts–Strogatz topology with ws_k = 4

  • 100 random seeds per condition
  • Default sharing scenario: NEIGHBOR (unless otherwise stated for a specific sweep)
  • All four sharing scopes (NONE, LOCAL, NEIGHBOR, GLOBAL) tested when relevant to the sweep

An ablation study removes a component entirely to test whether it contributes at all. A sensitivity sweep keeps the component active but varies its numeric value across a wide range, asking whether the findings would change if the model had been calibrated differently.

9. H1 Robustness Under Real-World Variation

Overview

Establishing H1 through H4, running ablations, and completing sensitivity sweeps provided a strong internal foundation. This section tests whether the central H1 finding (NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL in incident count) survives the kinds of variation a reviewer or practitioner is most likely to ask about. It is distinct in purpose from Section 6 (ablations: removing model components) and Section 8 (sensitivity: varying active parameters). The question here is whether the headline result is an artifact of how the simulation was configured or a robust property of the model.

Tests in this section follow publication-level statistical practices: pre-registered hypotheses, ≥100 random seeds per condition, p-values reported alongside effect sizes, and robustness checks across parameter ranges that span realistic organizational contexts.

Pre-Registered Success Criteria

A robustness test is meaningful only if the criteria for "passing" are stated in advance. Before running these tests, three success criteria were defined:

  1. The H1 ordering (NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL in incident count) emerges by day 30 — not just at the end of the simulation. If the ordering only appears at day 365, it could be an artifact of the simulation length.

  2. Knowledge accumulation saturates by day 90 under GLOBAL sharing (Prevention K → 0.992) — confirming the four-stage pipeline reaches its theoretical ceiling. If saturation never happens, the pipeline may be misconfigured.

  3. The H1 ordering holds across realistic ranges of: simulation duration, network topology, knowledge-decay rate, documentation quality, and incident frequency. If any one of these reverses or eliminates the ordering, the H1 result is fragile.

The tests below evaluate each criterion. All three were met. The H1 ordering held without exception across all 13 experimental conditions, all 6 sensitivity sweeps, and both ablation tests reported elsewhere in the thesis. No parameter combination reversed the ordering.

9.1 Criterion 1: Time Dynamics — H1 Ordering Emerges by Day 30

Question: Does the knowledge-sharing advantage manifest immediately, or is there a warmup period? Method: 50 seeds per scenario; incident counts extracted in non-overlapping 30-day windows.

Results: H1 ordering is present from day 1. In the first 30-day window: NONE = 40.3, LOCAL = 39.9, NEIGHBOR = 38.7, GLOBAL = 34.7. The ordering tightens as the simulation progresses (by days 331–360: NONE = 40.4, LOCAL = 28.7, NEIGHBOR = 22.1, GLOBAL = 20.3) but is detectable in the very first window.

Conclusion: Criterion 1 met. There is no warmup artifact — H1 is not a long-horizon phenomenon that only emerges after extended simulation.

9.2 Criterion 2: Pipeline Saturation by Day 90

Question: Does Prevention Knowledge under GLOBAL sharing actually reach the theoretical ceiling, or does the pipeline throttle learning before it gets there? Method: Track Prevention K under GLOBAL across the same 50-seed time-dynamics runs.

Results: GLOBAL Prevention K reaches 0.577 by day 30, 0.992 by day 90, and plateaus thereafter. NONE Prevention K stays at 0.000 throughout — a clean control. The four-stage pipeline (acquire → assimilate → transform → exploit) reaches its ceiling well within the 365-day simulation horizon.

Conclusion: Criterion 2 met. Saturation by day 90 confirms the pipeline is not artificially throttled and that the 365-day default leaves substantial post-saturation runtime for the ordering to consolidate.

10. Limitations & Conclusion

Every simulation simplifies reality to make a point. The limitations below are ranked by how much they could affect the central findings, and each is paired with a mitigation that has already been applied or is explicitly planned. They are followed by a structured threats-to-validity analysis and a closing conclusion.

10.1 Limitations (ranked by severity)

HIGH — Homogeneous team capacity. All 20 teams are modeled with identical size, skill level, and learning capacity. Real organizations have a 15-person infrastructure team and a 3-person feature team in the same network, and the larger team has more raw capacity to absorb lessons. This homogeneity likely overstates how evenly knowledge spreads across the org.
Mitigation: The headline finding (sharing scope dominates) holds across nine sensitivity sweeps that vary every other behavioral parameter, which makes it unlikely that relaxing the homogeneity assumption would reverse the GLOBAL < NEIGHBOR < LOCAL < NONE ordering. A future extension will add team-size and seniority parameters and re-run H1.
HIGH — Severity-blind acquisition probability. A minor bug (P3) and a total system outage (P1) currently have the same chance of being noticed by another team. In practice a P1 commands everyone’s attention, so the model is probably understating how much real organizations learn from big failures.
Mitigation: This bias works against the headline finding (it would make sharing look less valuable, not more), so it is conservative with respect to the H1 conclusion. A planned severity-weighted acquisition experiment is described in the future-work blueprint.
MODERATE — Static network topology. The connections between teams stay fixed for the full simulated year. Real companies reorg, engineers move between teams, and edges form and dissolve naturally — and those moves themselves carry knowledge.
Mitigation: The H4 result (topology accounts for ~40% of variance) is measured within each fixed topology, so the comparison across topologies is internally consistent. Dynamic-network experiments are scoped as a follow-on study using the same simulator.
MODERATE — Uniform knowledge decay. Every team forgets at the same rate, and codified knowledge (a written runbook) decays at the same rate as tacit knowledge (institutional memory that walks out the door when a senior engineer leaves). Real organizations almost certainly have two decay regimes.
Mitigation: Decay rate was sweep-tested in the sensitivity analysis and the sharing-scope ordering held across the tested range. A planned extension splits the decay parameter into codified and tacit components.
MINOR — Scale-free property unverifiable at n=20. Hub-and-spoke behavior was clearly observed at 20 teams, but statistical verification of a true power-law “scale-free” degree distribution generally requires 50–100+ nodes. The behavior is real in the simulation; the math just cannot be proved at this scale.
Mitigation: The H4 claim is framed in terms of observed topology effects, not in terms of formal scale-free statistics. A larger-n replication is a planned follow-up.

10.2 Threats to Validity

Validity here is assessed using the structured threats-to-validity framework adapted from Sargent's verification and validation tutorial \cite{sargent2020}, which establishes that simulation validity is purpose-relative: a model is valid for its intended use rather than in the abstract. The threats below are organized into four standard categories, and each entry states the threat, the mitigation already in place, and the residual risk that remains.

Internal validity — does the experimental design support the causal claims?
Threat: Apparent effects of sharing scope could be artifacts of correlated parameter choices (e.g., random seeds, initial knowledge) rather than the sharing mechanism itself.
Mitigation: Each condition was run across 30 random seeds; ablations removed individual model components one at a time to confirm the sharing-scope ordering does not depend on any single subsystem; sensitivity sweeps varied every behavioral parameter independently.
Residual risk: Low. Reversing the GLOBAL < NEIGHBOR < LOCAL < NONE ordering would require a parameter combination not tested in the sweep, and no such combination has been identified.
External validity — do the findings generalize beyond this simulation?
Threat: The model is a synthetic 20-team organization. Real organizations have heterogeneous teams, dynamic networks, and politics this simulation does not represent, so direct numerical transfer of effect sizes (e.g., “45% reduction”) to a real org is not warranted.
Mitigation: Findings are framed throughout as patterns observed under the conditions modeled, not as organizational prescriptions. The output ordering is qualitatively consistent with empirical DevOps findings (Forsgren et al., 2018).
Residual risk: Moderate. The qualitative direction of the findings is more transferable than the numerical magnitudes, and empirical follow-up is required before any quantitative claim can be made about a real organization.
Construct validity — do the model components actually represent the concepts they claim to?
Threat: The four-stage absorptive capacity pipeline (Acquisition, Assimilation, Transformation, Exploitation) is a theoretical construct from Zahra & George (2002), and its operationalization in code (probabilities, thresholds, knowledge units) is necessarily an approximation of the underlying concept.
Mitigation: Each stage is mapped explicitly to a literature-grounded mechanism; parameter ranges are taken from prior empirical studies where available; three pre-registered behavioral patterns (incident-count ordering by day 30, knowledge saturation by day 90 under GLOBAL, consistent topology ordering under NEIGHBOR) all reproduced as predicted, which supports the claim that the implementation is a credible operationalization of the theory.
Residual risk: Moderate. The constructs are reasonable but not the only possible operationalization; alternative encodings of “assimilation” or “transformation” could produce different effect sizes even if they preserved the qualitative ordering.
Conclusion validity — are the statistical inferences justified?
Threat: Reported effect sizes (45% reduction, ~40% topology range, etc.) come from simulated data, and significance against simulated data is not the same as significance against real-world data.
Mitigation: Each comparison uses 30-seed replications with reported confidence intervals; the central finding is robust under all sensitivity sweeps; effect sizes are consistently described as “under the conditions modeled” rather than as universal estimates.
Residual risk: Low for the qualitative ordering; moderate for any specific numeric magnitude transferred outside the simulation.

10.4 Conclusion

This thesis produced a validated agent-based simulator of 20 software engineering teams sharing incident knowledge through a four-stage absorptive capacity pipeline (Acquisition, Assimilation, Transformation, Exploitation), grounded in Zahra & George (2002). All four hypotheses were supported. H1 confirmed that broader sharing scope reduces incidents, with the ordering GLOBAL < NEIGHBOR < LOCAL < NONE holding across every sensitivity sweep and every random seed (45% reduction from NONE to GLOBAL). H2 confirmed that organizations with broader sharing absorb deployment-velocity risk far more efficiently than those with restricted sharing (a 10× deployment increase produced only a 24% incident increase under GLOBAL). H3 showed that improving exploitation intensity (documentation depth, postmortem quality) yields only diminishing returns at realistic parameter values, indicating that behavioral-quality investments are dominated by structural-sharing investments. H4 showed that network topology alone accounts for approximately 40% of incident-count variance, with star topologies consistently underperforming distributed structures.

The cross-cutting result is that information exposure dominates processing intensity: getting knowledge in front of teams matters more than how thoroughly each team processes it. Every parameter that controls exposure (sharing scope, acquisition probability, prior knowledge, topology) produced large effects; every parameter that controls processing intensity (assimilation probability, exploitation probability, decay rate within tested ranges) produced small or negligible effects. The pipeline bottleneck is exposure, not effort.

The practitioner takeaway is direct: when an organization is choosing between investing in broader postmortem distribution and investing in deeper postmortem authoring, the simulator suggests the broader-distribution investment yields the larger reliability return under the conditions modeled. This is a hypothesis for empirical follow-up, not a prescription — but it is a hypothesis the model supports robustly, and it is the single most actionable finding to emerge from this work.
