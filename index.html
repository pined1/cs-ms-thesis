1.1 The Central Problem

Software systems are inherently prone to failure. From server outages and database unavailability to broken production deployments, these events are a routine reality for organizations running software at scale. The factor that distinguishes a high-reliability organization from their peers is not whether they have incidents, but how they utilize those failures to improve their systems afterwards.

Most engineering organizations hold postmortems: structured retrospectives where teams document what went wrong, why it went wrong, and what they will change to prevent recurrence. A more nuanced and less understood question is whether the breadth of postmortem knowledge distribution actually impacts organizational outcomes.

If Team A experiences a database failure and writes a detailed postmortem, should only Team A read it? Should Team A's direct neighbors in the organization also read it? Should the entire company? Broader sharing intuitively seems better, but there is a real cost: meeting time, cognitive load, alert fatigue, and the risk of overwhelming teams with incidents that feel irrelevant to them. The tradeoff is real, and no rigorous simulation study has examined how different sharing strategies actually affect organizational reliability outcomes. This thesis addresses that gap.

1.2 Why This Question Matters

Despite industry-wide investment in postmortem processes, the literature on how sharing scope affects reliability outcomes has not been widely discussed. Organizational learning theory (Levitt & March 1988; Argote 1999) holds that knowledge must flow through an organization to be useful. Absorptive capacity theory (Cohen & Levinthal 1990; Zahra & George 2002) explains that receiving knowledge is not enough — teams must also have the capacity to absorb and apply it.

These two stages map onto a distinction this simulation takes seriously: acquisition versus assimilation. Acquisition is the moment a team reads a postmortem and registers that it exists and is relevant to them — they have seen it, logged it, flagged it as worth knowing about. That is all acquisition is. It does not mean the team understands the failure deeply or knows how to act on it. That understanding is assimilation, which is Stage 2 in this model and depends on the team's prior knowledge and absorptive capacity. A team can acquire a postmortem without ever truly assimilating it, and this distinction matters: the simulation models each stage separately because collapsing them would obscure exactly the dynamic we are trying to study.

These theories have not been tested in the specific context of software incident knowledge sharing with a formal computational model. This simulation study provides that test, bridging organizational learning theory and absorptive capacity theory in a setting grounded in real engineering practice.

1.3 What We Built: The Agent-Based Model

The model is reflective of an organization, with twenty software engineering teams arranged in a social network — some teams are adjacent, some are distant in their communication. Each team owns a subsystem of the software system, and those subsystems generate incidents over time according to a stochastic process every day. This study uses 20 teams as the base configuration; the model supports arbitrary team counts.

An analogy: a technology company with 20 engineering teams, each team owns a set of software services and those services experience outages. After each outage, the team writes a postmortem. Now, the question is: should the postmortem findings stay just within the team that experienced the outage, be shared with adjacent teams from their communication network, or broadcast across the entire engineering organization? Our model simulates exactly this decision, run 100 times over a simulated year, under four different sharing strategies.

The simulation was designed as an agent-based model (ABM), in which individual agents (teams) follow rules and their aggregate behavior produces emergent organizational outcomes. ABMs are well suited to this problem because they allow for the kinds of complex, unplanned patterns that arise in real organizations. This makes ABMs particularly useful for studying questions where the answer cannot be predicted from the rules alone.

Source Asymmetry and the Pipeline Delay

When an incident occurs on Team A, Team A is deliberately skipped from stages 2–4 of the learning pipeline. The rationale is straightforward: Team A already learned from living through the incident. Routing them through the standard acquisition-assimilation-application pipeline would double-count their learning and inflate their knowledge score artificially. This is a design decision, not a bug.

The flip side of this asymmetry is equally important: the source team's learning is immediate. They experienced the incident in real time, and their knowledge is updated on the same day it occurred. Every other team that learns from the postmortem must pass through all four pipeline stages before their behavior changes — and that takes time. This pipeline delay is intentional: it models the real organizational gap between "an incident happened somewhere in the org" and "our team has actually changed how we work because of it." Reading a postmortem is not the same as internalizing and applying it, and the four-stage pipeline enforces that distinction at every step.

The delay compounds with network distance. Under NEIGHBOR sharing, a team that is 2 hops from the source faces two compounding disadvantages: a lower acquisition probability (because signal decays with each hop) and the same full four-stage pipeline once they do acquire the postmortem. A team that is farther away is therefore both less likely to receive the knowledge and slower to act on it once received. This is not a model limitation — it is a structural feature that reflects how knowledge actually travels through hierarchical or loosely-connected organizations.

This asymmetry also explains one of the study's more counterintuitive results: under LOCAL sharing, the transformation rate is 0%. LOCAL means only the source team knows about the incident — and the source team is the one team excluded from the pipeline. There is no recipient left to transform the postmortem into applied knowledge.

One key mechanism governs how knowledge travels under NEIGHBOR conditions: signal decay. Each hop an incident postmortem travels through the network reduces the acquisition probability of the receiving team: 

p_acquire = acquisition_prob × signal_decay ^ path_length

Under GLOBAL sharing, every team is treated as path_length = 1 regardless of their actual network position — the signal does not decay with distance. Under NEIGHBOR sharing, only teams that are direct connections (path_length = 1) receive the postmortem at all, and the decay formula applies to those transmissions. Signal decay is introduced here because it is a structural property of the NEIGHBOR strategy; its effect on organizational outcomes is examined directly in H4.

1.4 The 4-Stage Absorptive Capacity Pipeline

The backbone of the learning model comes from the theory presented by Zahra & George's (2002) reformulation of absorptive capacity, which describes how organizations acquire, process, and use external knowledge. Rather than treating learning as a single binary event, the model implements a four-stage pipeline through which incident knowledge must pass before it changes a team's behavior.

Stage 1 — Acquisition: Does the team receive the incident knowledge? A team that is not in the sharing scope for an incident never even sees the postmortem. Teams within scope receive the incident with a probability that reflects their current communication network.

Stage 2 — Assimilation: Does the team that has passed the acquisition stage understand the knowledge? Even when a team receives a postmortem, they may not fully grasp its implications for their own systems. Assimilation probability is influenced by the team's existing knowledge base.

Stage 3 — Transformation: Does the team connect the new knowledge to what they already know? Transformation is the cognitive work of integration. This stage has the highest failure point in the model, reflecting real-world evidence that cross-domain knowledge transfer is difficult.

Stage 4 — Exploitation: Does the team actually change its behavior to reduce future incidents? This is the stage at which reliability outcomes are measured. A team that fully exploits an incident's lesson lowers the base incident rate for the relevant subsystems.

Taken together, the four stage probabilities define the organization's learning velocity — how fast incident knowledge travels from postmortem to behavioral change. A team with high acquisition probability but low transformation probability acquires many postmortems but converts few into structural improvements. An organization's overall learning velocity is the product of all four stage rates, which is why sharing scope (which determines who even enters Stage 1) dominates the other parameters: a team that never acquires a postmortem cannot benefit from high assimilation or exploitation rates.

A Note on H3 — Why Diminishing Returns?

In software reliability terms: moving a team from zero learning to some learning prevents a lot of incidents. Moving them from good to excellent at applying knowledge saves only a little more, because the easy wins are already gone, and the remaining risk is harder to eliminate.

2.1 The Central Question

Does sharing incident knowledge more broadly reduce system failures? H1 states: GLOBAL > NEIGHBOR > LOCAL > NONE, where "greater" means fewer incidents and higher availability. This section covers the evidence for that ordering and examines how robust it is across a wide range of conditions.

The expected ordering is NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL in total incidents (inverse ordering in knowledge accumulation). If any reversal appeared in these results, it would indicate a model implementation error. Confirming this ordering is the primary purpose of this experiment.

Intuition: The more teams that see an incident postmortem, the more teams enter the 4-stage learning pipeline. More teams in the pipeline means more teams eventually reach Stage 4 (Exploitation) and lower their incident rates. GLOBAL should produce the fewest incidents simply because it maximizes the number of pipeline entries per incident. If this ordering does not hold, the pipeline implementation is broken.

2.2 A Real-World Analogy: The Postmortem Sharing Decision

When a software organization has to handle a production outage, the on-call team has to jump in now to investigate, resolve the issue, and eventually write a postmortem. That document is full of critical lessons: which configuration was wrong, what monitoring was missing, and how the failure cascaded through the system. The real question is what happens to those lessons once the incident is over.

If the written report stays in the owning team's private Confluence page, that is local learning — the knowledge is isolated to the team that lived through the failure. If the team posts it in a shared Slack channel that adjacent teams in the communication network follow, that is neighbor-level learning — the signal reaches teams close in the organization graph. If the team that wrote the report presents their findings in a company-wide incident review attended by all engineering teams, that is global learning — every team hears about it and can update their own practices accordingly.

My simulation follows this same logic, using the organization as the primary unit of measurement. The core mechanism is agent-level knowledge accumulation, which directly reduces the probability of future incidents. By adjusting the dissemination boundaries, I can test how different sharing scopes impact the organization's overall reliability. Without broad sharing, teams keep repeating the same mistakes that neighboring teams have already solved.

2.6 The Transformation Rate Jump: Source Asymmetry

The 0% transformation rate in the LOCAL scenario is due to how LOCAL accumulates knowledge. In the simulation, the team that owns the affected subsystem is skipped in the post-incident learning pipeline — it is assumed to have learned implicitly by experiencing the failure. Under the LOCAL strategy, no other teams are in the learning pipeline, so the cross-team pipeline never fires and transformation events do not occur.

The NEIGHBOR strategy introduces at least one non-source team into every learning event. GLOBAL reaches 89.5% because every team in the organization received every incident's knowledge; cosine similarity then rises, the transformation threshold is crossed repeatedly, making structural learning the norm.

2.10 Conclusion

Under the conditions modeled — 20 teams, Watts-Strogatz topology, 365 simulated days, and a base incident rate of 0.10 — the data support H1. The ordering NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL in incident counts held across all 100 seeds and was statistically separable at each step (all Cohen's d > 3.44, p < 0.001). The 45% reduction from NONE to GLOBAL is large in absolute terms, but reflects specific model assumptions about how knowledge flows and decays. Whether this magnitude translates to real organizations depends on how well these assumptions hold in practice — a question for empirical follow-up. What the simulation establishes is that, under these assumptions, sharing scope is the dominant lever for reliability.

3.1 The Question

The question posed: does deploying software updates more frequently cause more incidents? The DevOps research literature (Forsgren et al., Accelerate, 2018) states a formal paradox — high performing organizations move faster and fail less. Our Hypothesis 2 investigates this tension directly, by asking whether the type of organizational learning moderates the relationship between a team's deployment velocity and incident counts.

H2 is confirmed if higher deployment rates produce more incidents, and if this effect is smaller under GLOBAL sharing than under LOCAL. A flat relationship between deployment rate and incident count would suggest the model is not capturing deployment risk correctly.

Intuition: More deployments = more chances to introduce a regression. Incident rate should rise with deployment frequency. However, under GLOBAL sharing, accumulated knowledge suppresses baseline risk — so the same deployment may produce fewer incidents than expected. The question is whether learning can outpace deployment risk.

3.2 A Real-World Analogy: Two Engineering Teams and Their Deploy Cadence

Consider two software engineering teams deploying bug fixes and feature updates to production regularly. Team A deploys once a week; Team B deploys every day or two. In the short run, Team B will encounter more incidents simply because they are pushing changes more often — more deployments yield more chances to introduce a regression into production code.

Now suppose Team B also learns faster. Every outage is a learning opportunity: the team writes a postmortem, identifies the root causes and key events, and updates their deployment checklist or automated tests (exploitation). The question is what happens to those lessons. If they stay with Team B, the team itself improves but the rest of the engineering organization keeps recurring the same mistakes. If the lessons are shared in incident reviews, the organization as a whole gets smarter, and future incidents become less likely across teams. The same number of deployments produces very different reliability outcomes depending on whether learning stays local or spreads globally.

3.3 What Deployment Rate Means in the Model

Each team is given a deployment rate parameter representing the probability of deploying on any given day. At 0.05, a team deploys roughly once every three weeks. At 0.50, a team deploys on approximately half of all working days. In the formula, a deployment risk multiplier of 1.5× is applied to incident probability on deployment days. 

p_incident = base_rate × (1 - avg_prevention × prevention_effect) × deployment_modifier

3.6 The Saturation Finding

Going from 0.05 to 0.50 represents a 10× increase in deployment frequency. Yet GLOBAL incidents rise only from 152.5 to 188.4, showing a near-flat GLOBAL curve.

When an organization has been operating under GLOBAL learning for a sustained period, Prevention K approaches 0.992. Substituting into the formula: 

(1 - avg_prevention × prevention_effect) ≈ (1 - K) = 1 - 0.992 = 0.008

Even when the deployment multiplier fires (1.5×), it multiplies against a residual risk of 0.008, because each team's accumulated knowledge has already suppressed most of the baseline risk. Under LOCAL sharing, teams never reach this state individually — knowledge stays within the team that experienced each failure and does not spread, so most teams across the organization remain underinformed and the deployment multiplier continues to act on a much higher baseline.

Saturation mechanism: Two rates are racing. Deployment velocity determines how fast incidents are generated. Learning velocity determines how fast knowledge accumulates to prevent them from occurring.

3.7 Connection to DORA and the Accelerate Research

Forsgren, Humble, and Kim (2018) identified a cluster of high performers characterized by high deployment frequency and low change failure rates. The H2 results offer one mechanism-level explanation consistent with this finding. When teams share incident knowledge broadly, organizational learning suppresses the baseline risk fast enough that deployment velocity no longer drives incidents upward proportionally. The simulation does not claim to fully explain the DORA correlation, but isolates the specific contribution of knowledge sharing structure to this relationship.

3.9 Conclusion

Under the conditions modeled, H2 is supported. Higher deployment rates produce more incidents across all conditions, confirming that deployment velocity is a source of incident risk in the model. However, the relationship is strongly sublinear under GLOBAL sharing: a 10× increase in deployment frequency (0.05 → 0.50) produces only a 24% increase in incidents. This is consistent with the learning saturation mechanism — when Prevention K approaches 0.992, the residual risk each deployment can trigger is near its floor. The DORA finding that high performers deploy frequently and fail less (Forsgren et al., 2018) is consistent with this mechanism, though the simulation isolates knowledge sharing as one specific contributor. Whether this saturation effect holds in real organizations depends on whether their learning pipelines approach the efficiency assumed here.

4. H3 — Learning Effectiveness (Exploitation)

H3 focuses on Stage 4 of the absorptive capacity pipeline — Exploitation. Recall from Section 1.4 that Stage 4 is where knowledge finally translates into behavioral change: a team updates their runbook, fixes a configuration, adds a monitoring alert. Stages 1–3 determine whether knowledge reaches a team and whether the team mentally integrates it. Stage 4 is where that mental integration becomes action.

In H1 and H2, exploitation probability (P_exploit = 0.60) was held constant. H3 asks a different question: how much does the intensity of Stage 4 effort matter? If we invest heavily in getting teams to follow through on postmortem action items — better tooling, accountability processes, dedicated time — does that produce proportional reliability gains?

A Real-World Framing

Imagine a VP of Engineering with a fixed budget and two options:

  • Option A - Structural: Build a global incident sharing platform so that every team can see every postmortem. This changes who has access to the incident knowledge.
  • Option B - Behavioral: Train teams in blameless postmortem facilitation, action item tracking, and follow-through accountability. This changes how intensely teams act on the knowledge they already have.

Theoretical Basis: Zahra & George (2002)

H3 targets Stage 4 (Exploitation Stage) of the absorptive capacity pipeline. The theoretical prediction is that exploitation does not yield high returns because the upstream stages act as a ceiling once knowledge reaches the exploitation stage. No matter how effectively a team exploits knowledge, they cannot exploit knowledge they never acquired or transformed.

First Run (100 seeds, range 0.0–0.10): The results appeared essentially linear across that narrow band. The problem was two-fold: the range was too narrow to reveal curvature, and at 100 seeds per condition, small effects are easily obscured by simulation variance.

Second Run (500 seeds, range 0.0–0.50): The range was extended five times and the seed count increased to 500 per condition, making this the most statistically rigorous test in the entire study.

Why Stage 4 Is Not the Bottleneck

A supplementary sensitivity sweep confirmed that varying exploitation probabilities (the likelihood that a team will attempt to apply knowledge) across the full range of 0.1–1.0 produces only a 3-incident variation across the entire simulated organization. The true bottleneck is at Stage 3 — Transformation — which is gated by cosine similarity between the team's existing knowledge profile and the new incident knowledge. Cosine similarity, in turn, is driven by the volume and diversity of knowledge a team has accumulated, and knowledge accumulation is driven by sharing scope.

Conclusion

Under the conditions modeled, H3 is supported with diminishing returns consistent with Zahra & George's (2002) pipeline theory. However, the absolute effect is modest. Moving from no exploitation effectiveness (0.0) to a realistic improvement (0.10) reduces incidents by approximately 4% — compared to 45% from expanding sharing scope in H1. This is not a failure of Stage 4; it reflects that Stage 3 (Transformation) is the actual bottleneck. A team cannot exploit knowledge it never transformed, and transformation is gated by the diversity and volume of knowledge accumulated — which is driven primarily by sharing scope. The practical implication under these model assumptions: structural changes to sharing scope (H1) should precede investments in exploitation quality (H3), because the ceiling on H3 returns is set by H1.

The model handles this through cosine similarity filtering. Each incident has a feature vector; each team has a competency profile. 6. Ablation Tests — exp11 (Knowledge Decay) & exp12 (Source Asymmetry)

6.1 What Is an Ablation Test?

In simulation and machine learning research, an ablation test is the standard methodology for isolating the contribution of individual model components: disable one component, re-run the full experiment under otherwise identical conditions, and measure what changes (Sargent 2020). Components that are load-bearing will produce meaningful shifts in output when removed; components that are incidental will leave the results largely intact. This section applies that methodology to two specific mechanisms in the H1 agent-based model — knowledge decay and source asymmetry — to determine whether the H1 ordering result is a genuine property of the sharing-scope architecture or an artifact of those auxiliary design choices.

The component being ablated in each experiment targets a distinct part of the agent learning pipeline. Knowledge decay (exp11) is a forgetting mechanism: by default, an agent's accumulated prevention knowledge erodes exponentially over time (decay rate = 0.001 per timestep), modeling staff turnover and practice atrophy. Source asymmetry (exp12) is a routing rule: when an incident occurs at Team A, Team A is excluded from stages 2–4 of the post-incident learning pipeline, on the premise that the source team already holds first-hand knowledge of the event. The diagnostic question for both ablations is the same: does the H1 ordering — NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL in cumulative incidents — emerge from the sharing-scope mechanism itself, or does it depend on one of these auxiliary components to hold? If removing decay collapses the ordering, decay is load-bearing. If the ordering survives, sharing scope alone is sufficient to produce it. Both experiments were run under the same conditions as the primary H1 experiment: 20 teams, Watts-Strogatz small-world network, 365 simulated days, 100 independent random seeds, all four scenarios.

6.2 exp11 — Removing Knowledge Decay

The default simulation treats prevention knowledge as impermanent. Knowledge earned by a team on day 10 can slowly erode by day 200 if it is not reinforced, following an exponential decay function (decay rate = 0.001 per timestep). This models engineers leaving teams, practices not being enforced, and on-call rotation. The empirical grounding is Darr, Argote, and Epple's (1994) study of organizational forgetting in the service industry, which tracked production knowledge across franchise locations and found that accumulated knowledge decayed measurably during idle periods when it was not being reinforced.

In exp11, the knowledge decay rate is set to 0.0; knowledge earned at any point is retained in full.

Why NEIGHBOR benefits most: Under the NEIGHBOR strategy, knowledge accumulates slowly, spreading outward from the source team one hop at a time over many timesteps. When decay is turned off, all that slowly accumulated knowledge is preserved in full. 6.3 exp12 — Removing Source Asymmetry

In the model, source asymmetry is one of the more important design decisions in the four-stage learning pipeline. When an incident occurs at Team A (the source team), Team A is skipped in stages 2–4 of the post-incident learning pipeline. The rationale is that Team A owns the affected subsystem and has experienced the incident in real time. Routing Team A through the standard learning pipeline for its own incident would double-count its learning.

In exp12, the source team skip is removed, meaning the source team now goes through all four pipeline stages for its own incident, exactly like any other learner.

6.4 Combined Conclusion: What exp11 and exp12 Together Establish

Together, the two ablations accomplish three things:

  1. They strengthen confidence in model validity. When a component is removed and results change in the direction and magnitude the model's logic predicts, that is evidence the model is behaving consistently with its own internal structure.
  2. They demonstrate robustness to design choices. Neither ablation reverses the H1 ordering. Under both ablations, the ordering (NONE > LOCAL > NEIGHBOR > GLOBAL) is a property of knowledge flow structure, not an artifact of decay rates or pipeline asymmetry.
  3. They provide mechanistic insight beyond the primary results. Exp12 illuminates why a LOCAL strategy exhibits a knowledge ceiling far below NEIGHBOR and GLOBAL under these modeled conditions.

7. Ablation — exp13 Learning Cost

7.1 The Central Question

Fewer incidents are desirable. But every postmortem review consumes engineering time. If global sharing substantially increases the number of learning events that occur, and each learning event carries a real time cost, could the overhead of learning outweigh the benefit of fewer failures?

Exp13 asks directly: does global knowledge sharing reduce total engineering cost, or does the learning overhead it generates cancel out the firefighting hours it saves?

7.2 Two Buckets of Cost

Cost Type 1 — Incident Response Cost (firefighting): When a production system fails, engineers drop what they are doing and restore it. Formula: 

engineering_cost_base (4 hrs) × severity_scale × duration_scale

Cost Type 2 — Learning Cost (investment): When teams move through the four-stage knowledge pipeline — reading postmortems, attending retrospectives, updating runbooks — they spend time tracked separately: 

learning_cost = 2.0 hours per successful learning event

More sharing produces more learning events, increasing learning cost. It also produces more knowledge capital, decreasing future incidents and firefighting cost. These two effects work against each other throughout the 365-day simulation.

7.3 How the Cost Dynamics Evolve Over Time

In the GLOBAL scenario, the learning cost is front-loaded. Prevention knowledge reaches near-saturation (k = 0.992) by approximately day 90. Incident rates drop sharply, and fewer incidents also mean fewer learning events, so learning overhead shrinks as well. By year's end, the learning investment was concentrated in those first 90 days, while the avoided-firefighting benefit accumulates across the remaining days of the simulation.

Core insight: The learning cost is front-loaded; the prevention benefit is persistent.

10. Limitations & Future Work

Every simulation simplifies reality to make a point. While these limitations don’t change the overall direction of my findings, they are important context for how the model can be sharpened for future research.

1. Relevance of Knowledge
Currently, the model assumes every incident has some value to every other team. In the real world, a database failure in one department might be totally irrelevant to a frontend team in another. This might slightly overstate the benefits of GLOBAL sharing.
Future Work: Add a “relevance floor” to filter out low-value knowledge transfers.
2. Network Scale
I observed clear “hub-and-spoke” behavior with 20 teams, but you generally need 50–100+ nodes to statistically verify “scale-free” properties. While the behavior is meaningful here, the math is harder to prove at this size.
Future Work: Re-run the network density tests (H4) with 50+ teams.
3. Incident Severity
In my model, a minor bug (P3) and a total system outage (P1) have the same chance of being noticed. In reality, a P1 commands everyone’s attention. This means the model might actually be understating how much we learn from big failures.
Future Work: Weight the “acquisition probability” based on how severe the incident is.
4. The “Urgency” Decay
A team is just as likely to read a post-mortem on day 50 as they were on day 1. Realistically, if you don’t read a report in the first week, you probably never will.
Future Work: Implement a “time window” where the chance of learning drops as the incident gets older.
5. Identical Teams
I treated all 20 teams as having the same size and skill level. Obviously, a 15-person infrastructure team has more “brain power” to absorb lessons than a 3-person feature team.
Future Work: Add parameters for team size and seniority to reflect varying learning capacities.
6. Static vs. Dynamic Teams
The connections between teams stay fixed for the whole year. In real life, companies reorg and engineers move between teams, which naturally carries knowledge with them.
Future Work: Allow the network to change over time (team members moving, edges dissolving).
7. Different Ways of Forgetting
The model assumes everyone forgets everything at the same rate. In reality, a written runbook stays forever, while “tribal knowledge” disappears the moment a senior dev leaves the company.
Future Work: Split “decay” into two parts: codified knowledge (long-lasting) and tacit knowledge (quick to fade).
8. Tracking Developer Hours
Right now, I apply a flat 2-hour cost to every learning event. To get a better ROI picture, I need to track exactly how many minutes are spent at each stage of the pipeline (Acquire vs. Transform vs. Exploit).
Status: Implementation is planned and will allow for much more precise cost attribution.

Part D: Conclusion

What We Built and Validated

This thesis produced a computational agent-based model of 20 software engineering teams sharing incident knowledge through a four-stage absorptive capacity pipeline — Acquisition, Assimilation, Transformation, and Exploitation — grounded in Zahra & George (2002). The simulation was not designed to prescribe organizational behavior; it was designed to provide a controlled experimental environment for studying how structural and behavioral variables interact in a knowledge-sharing system that would be difficult or impossible to manipulate in a live organization.

The model was pre-specified against three validation criteria before experiments were run: (1) H1 ordering emergence by simulation day 30, (2) Prevention Knowledge saturation by day 90 under the GLOBAL sharing condition, and (3) full topology ordering under the NEIGHBOR condition. All four experimental hypotheses were supported. The H1 ordering — GLOBAL < NEIGHBOR < LOCAL < NONE — held across 13 experimental conditions, 6 sensitivity sweeps, and 2 ablation tests. No parameter combination tested reversed this ordering.

The primary scientific contribution of this work is the simulator itself: a validated, reproducible computational tool that operationalizes absorptive capacity theory in a software incident learning context. The fact that the model reproduces theoretically predicted orderings provides evidence that it is a reasonable implementation of the underlying theory, and a credible basis for the exploratory findings described below.

What the Simulation Suggests

The following observations are specific to the model as implemented, under the assumptions encoded in it. They are offered as patterns worth investigating further, not as prescriptive conclusions.

  • Sharing scope (H1) is the dominant lever for reliability under the conditions modeled. Moving from no sharing to global sharing is associated with a 45% reduction in mean incident counts. This effect is large relative to other parameters tested and is robust to sensitivity variation in acquisition probability, exploitation effectiveness, and network density. These results are consistent with the theoretical claim that boundary-spanning knowledge transfer yields higher marginal returns than internal processing depth. Empirical follow-up is needed to determine whether this holds in real organizations and under what boundary conditions.
  • Network topology alone accounts for approximately 40% of variance in incident counts (H4), suggesting that organizational structure shapes learning outcomes independently of individual team behavior. Under these modeled conditions, star topologies consistently underperform distributed structures. This is suggestive of a structural bottleneck effect, though the mechanism has not been empirically confirmed outside the simulation.
  • Deployment velocity risk is strongly absorbed by organizational learning (H2). A 10× increase in deployment rate produced only a 24% increase in incidents under the GLOBAL sharing condition, compared to much larger increases under restricted sharing. This pattern is directionally consistent with findings from high-performing DevOps organizations (Forsgren et al., 2018), and suggests the simulator captures a plausible interaction effect. Whether this ratio holds empirically is an open question.
  • Stage 4 exploitation intensity (H3) shows diminishing returns at realistic parameter values. Under the range of exploitation effectiveness values tested (~0.3–0.7), the incident-count difference attributable to exploitation quality is approximately 4%. Under these modeled conditions, structural sharing investments appear to yield higher marginal returns than behavioral quality investments in documentation or postmortem analysis depth. This finding warrants caution before over-interpreting, as the model does not capture the full complexity of knowledge quality effects in real teams.
Summary: This study produces a validated computational implementation of absorptive capacity theory applied to software incident learning. Under the conditions modeled, sharing scope is the most robust predictor of reliability outcomes, and network topology matters independently of learning behavior. These patterns are suggestive and reproducible within the simulation; they are not organizational prescriptions. The open questions below identify the most promising directions for empirical follow-up and model extension.

11. Open Questions and Future Directions

The simulation findings described in this thesis are best understood as a set of structured hypotheses generated by a validated computational model, not as empirical conclusions about real organizations. This section translates the most policy-relevant simulation patterns into candidate empirical hypotheses, proposes specific follow-up experiments that could be run using this simulator, and identifies extensions that would increase the model's realism and scientific scope.

11.1 From Simulation Findings to Empirical Hypotheses

Each of the following patterns was observed consistently within the simulation under the conditions modeled. Each represents a testable claim about organizational behavior that existing simulation data cannot confirm on its own.

  • Sharing scope vs. documentation quality (from H1). The simulation suggests that broadening sharing scope yields higher marginal returns than improving the depth or quality of individual postmortem documentation. This could be tested with natural experiments comparing organizations that changed their postmortem distribution policies — for example, organizations that moved from team-local wikis to company-wide incident databases — while holding documentation format and training investments roughly constant. If the simulation pattern holds in practice, incident rates should decline more after policy changes that expand audience than after changes that improve documentation standards.
  • Hub-spoke topology and knowledge diffusion (from H4). The simulation suggests that star-topology organizational designs produce systematically worse knowledge diffusion than distributed structures. This is testable using organizational network analysis data collected from software engineering teams with identifiable knowledge-sharing structures. Organizations with high betweenness centralization in their communication graphs would be predicted, under this hypothesis, to show slower incident-learning curves than those with more distributed connectivity.
  • Deployment velocity and learning absorption (from H2). The simulation suggests that high-deployment organizations with strong knowledge sharing may absorb deployment risk more efficiently than those with restricted sharing. This is directionally consistent with DORA findings (Forsgren et al., 2018) linking elite performers to both high deployment frequency and low change failure rates. The mechanism proposed by the model — faster incident-knowledge propagation buffering the risk of each additional deployment — warrants mechanism-level empirical study, including longitudinal analysis of organizations undergoing CI/CD adoption alongside postmortem practice changes.

11.3 Model Extensions

The following extensions would increase the simulator's scientific credibility and expand the range of questions it can address:

  • Formal ODD protocol documentation. The model should be documented using the ODD (Overview, Design concepts, Details) protocol (Grimm et al., 2020) to support independent replication and submission to venues such as the Journal of Artificial Societies and Social Simulation (JASSS). The current documentation is sufficient for thesis purposes but does not meet the reproducibility standard expected for computational social science publication.
  • Scale-free validation with 50+ nodes. The current 20-team implementation produces Barabási-Albert topologies that are too small to exhibit true scale-free degree distributions. Rerunning experiments with 50 or more nodes would allow proper empirical testing of BA topology properties and more reliable H4 topology comparisons.
  • Heterogeneous teams. The current model assigns all teams identical base competencies and sizes. Introducing variation in team size, prior knowledge levels, and incident exposure rates would better reflect real software organizations and could reveal interaction effects not visible in the homogeneous baseline.
  • Dynamic knowledge decay. The model currently does not distinguish between codified knowledge (documented in postmortems) and tacit knowledge (held by individual engineers). Implementing differentiated decay rates for each type — codified knowledge decays more slowly but is less actionable; tacit knowledge decays faster with attrition — would address a known limitation of the absorptive capacity operationalization and make the model more sensitive to realistic turnover scenarios.
